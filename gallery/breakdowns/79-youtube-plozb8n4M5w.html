Craft

A 62-second Cognition film for Devin Bots, built around Sage Cadence, a Stanford electrical engineering student who makes circuit boards and posts about them. It is set in one room at one table, covered from eight camera positions, with two graphic sequences made from her own short videos. She explains what the bots do in her own words: one researches parts, one writes firmware, one checks her work.

It is worth studying for its coverage and for how it moves between camera footage and interface. A cluttered bench is dressed so that every angle has a foreground, and one high wide from the corner works as the map you return to. The interface comes into the film in steps: first a flat window of her videos over a chat, then a chip over the footage, then name tags placed on the table, then the logo growing between her hands.

Story and structure

An outtake as the cold open

The first words in the film are a question meant for the crew: 'Also, where should I look?' The edit keeps it and answers it with the next cut to a look straight down the lens. Four seconds later she laughs and says 'Sorry.' mid-introduction, and that stays in too. Before the product is mentioned, the film has told you this is a real person who was not reading lines, and it has done it with footage most edits would throw away.

The structure is short and plain. Who she is (0:03), what she already makes, shown through her own channel (0:10) and three star boards (0:18), how long that used to take (0:26), the bots (0:40), one line for each of the three (0:41 to 0:52), and a summary in her words: 'It's kind of like having a little engineering team of my own.' The product description on YouTube quotes the same line. Her own projects carry the specifics: a heart-shaped board, a portable voice diary called lyra, a board that lights up in the dark called cassiopeia, and a third star that is 'a secret'.

How the frames work together

Eight angles around one table

The whole film happens at one table, and the coverage is planned so it never looks like the same place twice in a row. The table is dressed like a skyline: vintage test gear stacked high (a scope under a bench meter, an analog power supply, a soldering station), a pile of books with boards on top, a clear parts box, green and blue boards, a blue grid cutting mat with the laptop and an orange multimeter. Every angle has something of it in the foreground. The opening wide puts a stack of gear on each side of her like bookends; the low shots put a grey tool or the orange meter big and soft in front of the lens; the inserts sit right down on the mat among the parts.

The angles fall into a few families, each with its own job. Across the table at eye level is for presenting: the opening wide, the interview to the lens, and the moment she holds up the star. The three-quarter interview, with her eyeline just past the lens, carries the explaining after 0:25, so she is no longer selling to you but talking to someone. Beside her at table height, along her arms to the laptop, is the working angle: typing and hands. Table-level macros and one straight-down shot show the making. The laptop screen at a raking angle shows the bots' answers without ever going to a flat screen recording.

The high wide from the corner is the anchor. It is the only shot that shows the room (glazed door, boombox, walnut credenza, a window and sun across the floor, a striped rug under the table), and it is held back until 0:34, more than halfway in. It is used four times, each at a turning point: time compressed into smears for 'trims down the time to success', the look up for 'Look, it's already done', the name tags for 'Meet my Devin Bots', and the logo. She speaks to it twice on camera, so the overview is also a character in the room.

The cutting follows her phrases, not the music. Shots average about two seconds (median 1.5) and are shortest from 0:30 to 0:40, eight cuts in ten seconds, across 'build it and then check it' and 'trims down the time to success'. The beat check gives no lock: 5 of 29 detected cuts fall within 70 ms of a beat, exactly what chance would give.

Showing the product

The interface moves into the room

The interface enters the film in four steps, and each one sits closer to the room than the last. At 0:10 it takes over the whole frame: a dark chat with her bots fills the background, and her own vertical videos play in a fixed 9:16 window in the middle. The camera footage is gone. At 0:21 the window comes back, but the background is a defocused shot of her bench, so the window now floats in her room. At 0:31 the interface is only a small frosted chip in the corner of camera footage. At 0:40 the bots' name tags are placed on the table itself, among the gear, and at 0:57 the logo grows between her hands in the same shot.

The hand-offs out of the graphics are matched. The first window cuts to a shot straight down on the table, the one camera angle as flat as a screen: laptop, grid mat, striped rug and coiled cable read as shapes, the way the chat did. The second window ends on star stickers placed over her face to name each board, and the next shot has the real teal star clipped in her hair. The flat version is shown first and the physical one replaces it.

The bots are introduced the way she was. Her name came up at 0:03 as a frosted chip at bottom left, name over role. Caesar arrives at 0:31 as the same chip in the same place, name over task, 'Build my hardware's software'. It is held across four shots and three angles, so the task stays while the camera moves. Later the screen shots pay off something planted early: the voice diary prompt typing in the chat behind her clips at 0:14 is typed in full on the real laptop at 0:37 and sent at 0:39.

The product is never shown as a flat recording. Every screen is shot on the laptop at a raking angle, with her fingers soft in front and the keys in the corner, so the interface keeps the same light and grain as everything else.

Music and sound

The music stops for 'Sorry.'

The first six seconds have no music, only her voice and the room, which suits an outtake. The music hits at 0:06.4 under 'who loves to build hardware', and at 0:07.9 it is cut dead as she breaks and says 'Sorry.' Three seconds of room sound follow, under 'That I also share on the internet' and her smile into the lens, and the music slams back at 0:11.0 with the first of her own videos. It treats her laugh like a record scratch, and it makes the cut into her channel the start of the film proper.

From there the music runs at an even level under her voice to the end, carrying the two wordless seconds of soldering at 0:15–0:18 and dipping once for under a second at 0:38 while she types the prompt. It fades under the logo. The ten-second stretch of her clips uses their own audio, so her channel voice ('Hi, friends', 'Make your first circuit board with me') and her interview voice sit side by side.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Keep the outtake that proves it is real

    Open on a question meant for the crew and answer it with the next cut. A laugh and a 'Sorry.' do more for trust than any line about being authentic.

  2. Hold the room back

    Shoot one high wide that shows the whole space, save it until halfway, and use it only at turning points. It becomes the map the rest of the coverage hangs on.

  3. Dress the table for every lens

    Arrange gear in stacks and piles at different heights so each camera position finds its own foreground: bookends for the wide, a soft tool for the low shot, parts around the macro.

  4. Bring the interface in by steps

    Start with a full-frame graphic, then a window over the real room, then a chip over the footage, then labels placed in the scene. The product moves from a screen into the person's world.

  5. Give the product a lower third like the person's

    Introduce each bot with the same chip, in the same place, as the human, and hold it across cuts so a task stays on screen while the angles change.

  6. Match the flat to the flat

    Leave a flat graphic on a straight-down shot, and swap a sticker for the real object it stood for. The eye reads the change as one move.

  7. Plant the prompt early

    Let the prompt type in the background of an early graphic, then type and send it on the real laptop twenty seconds later.

Not the lesson here. It does not explain how Devin Bots work, show a setup, or put a number on the time saved. There is no narrator, no product walkthrough and no call to action on screen; the description carries the link.

Motion

Almost all the movement in this film is in the cutting, not in the camera. Most setups are locked off or barely drift, and a 62-second film has 32 shots across eight positions around one table, plus two graphic sequences. The moves worth studying are the hand-offs: from an outtake to a look down the lens, from the room into a flat window of her own videos and back out into the flattest camera angle, from a sticker star to the real star in her hair, and the interface's steady move into the room, from a full-screen chat to a chip over the footage to name tags placed on the table. The sequences below sample those changes.
