How 3 Students Built a €130K ARR AI Learning System

Lovable · 02:21 film

Watch original ↗

A Lovable founder story about Isabel, Andreas and Eric, three Swedish students who say they dropped out to build Klar, an AI learning tool, and reached €130,000 ARR in its first month. Interviews in one warehouse set are cut against a scrapbook of polaroids, archive engravings and highlighted documents on black paper, and most of the speech is typeset on screen word by word. The breakdown follows how the captions become the graphics system, how a 1088 date and a university letter carry the argument, and how the music steps out for the personal lines.

Craft

A two-and-a-half-minute founder story for Lovable about three students who say their university tried to stop them, and who built Klar, an AI learning tool, anyway. The interviews are plain: three people in director's chairs in one warehouse set. Everything around them is built from typography and paper: most of what they say is set on screen as they say it, and the narrator's lines play out on a black paper surface of polaroids, archive engravings, highlighted documents and antique maps.

What is worth learning is how much warmth a caption system can carry. Numbers are set big, feelings are set in an italic serif, an aside sits in parentheses, and pictures take the place of words. Two story props carry the argument from start to end: a year, 1088, that becomes 2026 without anything changing, and a warning letter from the university that comes back at the end with new words.

Story and structure

A founder story with two props

The shape is the familiar one: a hook, the people, the problem, why it is personal, the leap, the product, the traction, a human payoff and a final turn. What makes it hold together in two and a half minutes is that the opening seventeen seconds already contain the ending. The narrator says they were almost banned, Eric gives the number, and Isabel says the university called them in. The viewer spends the rest of the film waiting to hear how that turned out.

Two props carry the argument. The first is a date. '1088' sits alone on the black paper, archive images gather around it, and then it becomes '2026' while the images stay where they are. The claim that the system has not changed in a thousand years is made by not moving anything. The second is the warning letter, which slides into Isabel's shot in the hook and returns at the end with the same letterhead and new words: 'Actually, we take that back.'

The film also closes where it opened. The black-and-white portrait of the three of them at the start comes back in colour for the last line, 'all it took was three friends to change it', and the archive wall from the 1088 table passes by on the way there. Apart from the title card, nothing in the ending is new; it is the opening, answered.

  2. 1088 has become 2026 and nothing on the table has moved.

  3. The same letter, now reading 'Actually, we take that back.'

  4. The opening portrait returns in colour for the last line.

Voices and script

A narrator for the structure, founders for the feeling

There are two kinds of voice. An unseen narrator speaks in the third person ('they dropped out', 'they left their lives behind') and carries everything that needs to be told quickly: the hook, the introductions, the history, the leap and the reversal. The three founders are interviewed on camera and carry the things only they can say: the number, the ADHD, the vision, the thank-you message. The narrator is never seen and never named.

The lines are cut short and handed back and forth. A founder rarely speaks for more than ten seconds before the narrator or another founder takes over, and the handoffs often finish each other's thoughts. Andreas asks whether books and lectures are the best way to learn and answers himself, 'I don't think so, personally', and the narrator picks up with 'The year is 1088'. Eric says 'everyone learns differently' and his line continues over the light shapes. The narrator's 'it was time to turn Klar from an idea into reality' hands straight to Isabel's 'I've always had these big visions'.

Each founder has a job. Eric is the numbers and the product: €130,000, the list of formats. Isabel is the personal stake and the business: the ADHD, Lovable, the partnership. Andreas is the questioner and the warmth: the opening doubt, the markets, the student who passed. In the interviews each of them sits alone; they appear together only in their snapshots and in the two portraits that bookend the film.

  1. Andreas answers his own question, and the narrator takes it into history.

  2. Isabel's ADHD: a line only she could say.

  3. The narrator takes the story back for the reversal.

The world and its graphics

Captions as the graphics system

Most of the speech is set on screen as it is said, one word at a time, in small white sans placed in the empty space beside the speaker. That is the base layer, and the design comes from how it bends. Numbers are set three times larger: '€130,000 ARR', '35,000 students'. The word that carries feeling switches to a serif, usually italic: 'forcing', 'old fashioned', 'everything', 'good', 'ADHD'. An aside shrinks and goes into parentheses: '(personally)'. 'REINVENT' is stamped in a rough typewriter face. The layout changes with the frame, a vertical column beside Isabel, a scatter around Andreas, a single word at a time at the left of the wide, so the captions feel placed by hand rather than generated.

Off camera, everything lives on one surface: black paper with a faint grid, dust and soft light. Polaroids stack on it between a pair of parentheses, as if the photos were an aside in the sentence. Archive engravings and paintings gather on it around the year. Web pages are printed and laid on it with a yellow marker line. The university letter slides across it. Even the antique maps sit in a rounded lantern-slide frame against black. The palette stays cream type on near-black throughout, which lets the few colour photographs and the yellow highlights stand out.

Pictures can also take a word's place. 'thousands of [a cut-out crowd of students] were waiting to learn differently'; 'it was time to turn [a laptop full of code] from an idea into reality'. The narrator says the word and the screen shows the thing. Klar's own mascot, the cream cloud with star eyes from the product's welcome card, peeks into the corner of the full-frame type, so the product's character lives in the film's graphics, not just in a screenshot.

  1. The number set three times the size of the caption around it.

  2. Polaroids held inside parentheses, one per name.

  3. Grotesk and pixel-edged italic serif, with Klar's mascot in the corner.

  4. A cut-out crowd stands in for the word 'students'.

  5. Antique maps as lantern slides, still on black.

How the frames work together

One set, two angles, and a camera that shows itself

All three interviews are shot in the same place: a director's chair on a worn rug in a warehouse with peeling painted walls, tall windows, a ladder, candles and plants. Each founder has a locked-off wide, centered and symmetrical, and a tight side angle with the background soft. The wide leaves room for the captions to sit in the air; the close is used for the short line that matters, such as 'I don't think so', 'I have ADHD' or 'everyone learns differently'.

Twice the film lets the production show. At 1:09 Andreas is framed between two cinema cameras and their monitors with a boom mic over his head, and at 1:28 Isabel is seen past a soft dark shape in the foreground that looks like a crew member holding a monitor. These shots make the set feel like a real day with real people rather than a polished ad, and they cost nothing to include.

Colour is held back as a bookend. The portrait that opens the film is black and white; the same pose closes it in colour.

  1. The wide: chair, rug, warehouse, room for type.

  2. Cameras and a boom mic left in the frame.

  3. Isabel past what looks like a crew member's shoulder.

Music and sound

Music that steps out for the personal lines

The soundtrack is a music score with no credited track, and by the stems it is built in sections that follow the story. A bass-heavy cue opens under the portrait, a pulse runs under the hook, and rising sweeps end on the cuts into the scrapbook (17.8), the desk (25.2) and Andreas's question (27.9). The tempo estimate is about 157 BPM, which may be double the felt pulse, and the cuts do not follow the beat more than chance would give.

The placement that is worth learning is where it stops. The music drops out under Andreas's question from about 29 to 33.5 seconds, comes back as '1088' appears, swells under the dropout statistic, and stops dead just before Isabel says 'I have ADHD'. Her line plays almost dry for six seconds, and the music returns only on Eric's 'everyone learns differently'. The two most personal statements in the first minute are the two places with no score.

From about 1:25 the busy rhythmic section gives way to a calm, steady bed under the building, the traction and the thank-you story, with small hits as the first map and the Spain map arrive. A big low hit lands on the narrator's 'and now' at 2:10, a rising build carries the reversal, and it stops on 'change it' before a short coda under the title.

  1. No music under Andreas's question.

  2. Isabel's ADHD line, with the score cut out.

  3. The big hit on 'and now' starts the final build.

Trust, warmth and craft

Specifics, admissions, and where it is reconstructed

Most of the credibility comes from the founders themselves. They give specific numbers (€130,000 ARR in the first month, a partnership covering 35,000 students) and the film sets those numbers big enough that they are hard to miss. Isabel says she has ADHD and that school was 'very tough' even with good grades. Andreas laughs while telling the thank-you story. These are things a script would not invent, and the film keeps them.

The warmest anecdote is also the plainest stretch. From about 1:57 to 2:08, while Andreas describes students writing in to say 'I had such a bad teacher and I still passed because of you guys', there are no captions and no graphics, only the wide and the close. After so much type, the absence makes it feel like a moment overheard rather than produced.

It is worth being clear about what is reconstructed. The university letter is a stand-in with a generic letterhead ('THE UNIVERSITY', 'Dear Student'), not a real document, and some of the dorm snapshots carry date stamps from 1998 and 2005, so they illustrate the dorm rather than show it. The numbers, the near-ban and the university's change of heart are the film's claims, told by the founders and the narrator, and are not sourced on screen.

  1. The headline number, as the founders state it.

  2. Dorm snapshots whose date stamps show they are stand-ins.

  3. The thank-you story told with no captions at all.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Caption the speech, then break the rule on purpose

    Caption speech word by word in one plain style, then use scale for numbers, an italic serif for the feeling word and small parentheses for asides. The exceptions become the emphasis.

  2. Put a picture where a word would be

    Let the narrator say the noun while the screen shows it inline in the sentence: a crowd for 'students', a laptop of code for the product.

  3. Show the problem by not changing the picture

    Gather old images around a date, change only the date, and add the new images to the same pile. The stillness makes the claim.

  4. Plant a prop in the hook and turn it at the end

    Introduce one document or object early with a clear meaning, and bring it back with that meaning reversed for the payoff.

  5. Pull the music out for the confession

    Drop the score entirely under the most personal lines and bring it back on the line that turns the feeling into the idea.

  6. Let the warmest story play plain

    After a dense graphic language, remove the captions and graphics for the most human anecdote so it reads as unproduced.

  7. Bookend in black and white, then colour

    Open on a monochrome portrait of the people and close on the same pose in colour, so the ending feels like the opening completed.

Not the lesson here. The film barely shows the product: one glimpse of Klar's welcome card, a row of feature labels and a laptop of code, and Lovable itself appears only as a word in a caption and a logo. There are no students or partners on camera and no sources for the numbers, so it is not a film to study for product demos or evidence. It is a study in typography, story props and pacing around three people talking.

Motion

The movement is almost all typographic. Most spoken lines appear on screen word by word as they are said, placed in the space beside the speaker, sized up for numbers and switched to an italic serif for the word that carries feeling. Between the interviews, the graphics live on one black paper surface: polaroids stack inside parentheses, archive images gather around a date, documents slide up with a highlighted line, and pictures take the place of words in a sentence. The sequences below sample those moves, the stop-motion light shapes, the lantern-slide maps and the closing title.
