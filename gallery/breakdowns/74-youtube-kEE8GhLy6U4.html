Craft

A forty-one second launch spot for Arc's iPhone app that is mostly not about the app. It is a montage of borrowed film clips (people alone with laptops at night, a snail, Mr. Bean packing, the Little Miss Sunshine van, the flying house from Up, the footbridge race from Jules and Jim) under one sentence spread across nine seconds of black cards and captions: 'Home. What if you could bring home with you? You can. Your home on the internet is now in your pocket.' The product appears for about seven seconds, as a smiley, a few lists and an icon.

What is worth learning is how far an idea can be carried by other people's pictures. Arc had already called itself a home on the internet, and 'home' is the only idea the film develops: first as screens people live in, then as something you pack and take with you. The clips do the emotional work, the sentence does the arguing, and the app's own words ('Hi there', 'Let's go', a space named Home) are cut in as if they were lines in the film.

Story and structure

One word, turned over twice

The whole film is built on 'home'. It opens by showing the internet as the place people already live: a laptop between a pizza slice and a plastic bag, faces lit blue in dark rooms, someone asleep at the desk. 'Home' over those shots is half joke and half true, and it borrows the idea Arc had already used for itself on the desktop.

Then the word turns. 'What if you could bring home with you' changes home from a place you are stuck in to something you can carry, and the clips change with it: a snail, suitcases, a tent, a van, a bus with luggage on the roof, and finally a house lifted by balloons. By the time the sentence resumes with 'Your home on the internet is now in your pocket', the viewer has already seen a dozen ways to take a home along, and the app only has to be the last one.

The answer is very short. 'You can' sits on black for under a second, and the product follows straight away with its own greeting. The film spends twenty-three seconds on the question and wish, and about seven on the app.

  2. 'What if', on a man looking around an empty room.

  3. The snail: a home on its back.

  4. 'You can', alone on black.

How the frames work together

A borrowed montage with a house of its own

Almost every shot comes from somewhere else: a sitcom, an indie road movie, Pixar, the French New Wave, footage of strangers. They are not regraded to match. Black-and-white clips stay black and white, a 4:3 Mr. Bean shot keeps its black bars, and the bicycle shot is pillarboxed. The mix of formats is part of the look, closer to a fan supercut or a moodboard than to an ad shoot, and it suits a product whose users collect things from all over the web.

The cutting follows the idea, not the beat. The audio check finds 7 of 30 cuts within 70 ms of the music's beat, about what chance would give. Instead the shots get shorter as the packing speeds up (down to 0.58 s around 15.6 to 16.8) and then the film stops on the Up window for 3.8 seconds, its longest shot, so the flying house gets time to land.

Up is used twice, and that is the structural trick. The first time, the house passes a window and a family stares. The second time, at the end, it rises between buildings with the Arc logo drawn over it. The borrowed image that carried the idea comes back carrying the brand.

  1. Mr. Bean letterboxed in 4:3, left as found.

  2. The Little Miss Sunshine van, near the fastest part of the montage.

  3. The longest shot: the house drifts past a window.

  4. Up again, now with the Arc logo over it.

Voices and script

A sentence spread across the cuts

There is no narrator. The script is one sentence, broken into six pieces and written in a plain white serif: 'Home', 'What if', 'you could bring home with you', 'You can', 'Your home on the internet', 'is now in your pocket', then 'Arc for iPhone'. Each piece is either alone on black or laid across footage like a subtitle, and when it is on footage it usually holds through a cut, so the words feel steadier than the pictures.

The serif matters. It reads like a film title or a book, not like app marketing, and it sets the borrowed clips up as cinema. The only sans serif type in the film belongs to the app's own screens.

Two spoken lines come from the clips, 'What's out there?' and 'Where's my phone?', and both happen to be the question the film is asking. Later the app speaks for itself: its onboarding says 'Hi there' and 'Let's go', and the edit treats those as lines of dialogue, cutting from 'Let's go' straight to three friends running.

  1. 'Home' holds while the clip under it changes.

  2. The caption sits across a blur, like a subtitle.

  3. The app's 'Let's go', used as a line.

  4. Jules and Jim answer it by running.

Showing the product

Seven seconds of product, all of it personal

The app gets three short passages: the onboarding greeting, a swipe through three spaces, and the home screen icon. None of them explains a feature. The screens are shown small and unframed on black, without a phone around them, so they read as one more image in the montage rather than as a demo.

What they show is chosen to fit the theme. The spaces have their own colors and human names (Browser co, Crafting, Home), and the items in them are specific and a little private: 'Crochet Stitches', 'Starting a Sashiko', 'Home buys', 'June trip', 'Murakami's vinyl collection'. The last space is literally called Home. A viewer sees someone's interests, not a tab manager.

The icon passage does two jobs at once. Arc's icon flickers through its alternate designs between Spotify and Messages, which shows off a small feature and also places Arc on the home screen among the apps people already keep there.

  2. A swipe from 'Browser co' to 'Crafting'.

  3. The Home space, with 'Murakami's vinyl collection'.

  4. The icon flickering between Spotify and Messages.

Music and sound

Quiet question, loud answer

The music is the only designed sound, and it has one big move. For the first seven and a half seconds it sits around -28 dB under the lonely-laptop clips and the first spoken line. At 7.75, as 'Where's my phone?' is heard and just before the cut to the teal backpack, the music stem jumps about 15 dB and stays loud for the rest of the film. The same moment is the switch from black and white to color. The question is asked quietly and the packing starts at full volume.

A sung vocal enters at about 19.5, as the Up shot begins. Its words are not clear enough to transcribe, but its breaths are visible in the voice track, and the black title cards sit near them: the vocal drops out from about 23.0 to 24.3, almost exactly the second 'You can' is on screen, and there are similar gaps around 28.0 to 29.0 and 32.3 to 33.5, close to the next two cards. The cards are placed where the singer leaves room.

  2. The music jumps to full level on the cut to color.

  3. The song's vocal comes in with Up.

  4. 'You can' in a pause in the vocal.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Develop one word, not a feature list

    Pick the word your brand already owns (here 'home') and spend the film turning it over: first what it means now, then what it could mean. The product only needs to be the last example.

  2. Let found footage carry the feeling

    Clips everyone half-remembers bring their own emotion. Choose them for one shared action (packing, leaving, floating) and keep their original formats instead of cropping them to match.

  3. Hold the words still across cuts

    Let a caption stay in place while the shots change beneath it. The words become the steady thread and the clips become examples of them.

  4. Cut the product's own copy in as dialogue

    Use the interface's real words ('Hi there', 'Let's go') as lines, and answer them with footage, so the app feels like a character instead of a demo.

  5. Make the demo content personal

    Fill the screens with specific, human item names and a space called Home. Viewers read a person's interests, which carries the warmth the montage built.

  6. Bring the borrowed image back with your mark on it

    Reuse the clip that best stood for the idea as the end card, with the logo laid over it, so the metaphor and the brand meet in one frame.

  7. Hit the music at the turn

    Keep the score low under the setup and let it jump at the exact moment the film turns from problem to wish, together with a change in the picture.

Not the lesson here. There is no narrator, no on-camera person from the company, no feature explanation and almost no original footage. Its cuts are not timed to the beat, and the clips are not color-matched. Study it for building a launch film around one word and other people's pictures.

Motion

The film has almost no movement of its own. Nearly every moving thing is borrowed: people packing, running, pushing a van, a house on balloons. What the editors control is the cut and the type. Shots average about 1.3 seconds and get shorter through the packing montage, the white serif sentence either holds still across cuts or stands alone on black, and the few designed moves are the app's own: a smiley that blinks, spaces swiped sideways, an icon flickering through its variants, and a logo outline laid over the last shot. The sequences below follow those cuts and the type across them.

Each sequence below is sampled at a fixed rate so its frames read as a flip book. Bars show how much the picture changes from one source frame to the next: tall bars are fast moves, flat stretches are holds. The same notes are in motion.md for reading as text.
