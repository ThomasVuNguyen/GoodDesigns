# Craft notes: Some Ideas Are Too Loud to Ignore

Lovable · 1:00 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ADXWl44apao

A minute about an idea you cannot shake, told as a song that will not leave a woman alone. It is in the street as she walks past the flats, in the bookshop where everyone seems to be keeping time, on the cover of a picture book, in the aisle, out of the window of a black cab, and finally in her kitchen, where the band is waiting. Only then does she open a laptop and type 'Build an app for the songs in my head'.

What is worth studying is how the music is used as the idea itself. The film was made with a real band (@bokoyout in the description, 'Boko Yout' on screen), and the mix is edited like a camera: muffled when the song is somewhere else, stripped to a tick when it is in her head, slammed back on a cut when she faces it, and made small when the band has gone and only the thing she built is playing it. Her face tells the same story, from irritation to giving in.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## An earworm, told in stages

The structure follows how a tune gets stuck. First it is just around: a stranger's earbuds, posters, a radio on the pavement. Then it is inside her: the shop goes quiet and everything seems to keep its rhythm. Then it takes a face: the book cover grows the band, and the singer rises out of the aisle. Then it follows her home. Each stage moves the music closer to her, and the film never needs to explain the metaphor because it keeps acting it out.

Her face carries the arc. She frowns past the posters, closes her eyes at the counter, winces at the cover, jumps when the singer appears, backs away, and then, after the cab passes, smiles. That smile at 40 seconds is the turn. Everything before it is resistance; everything after it is her giving the idea what it wants.

The product arrives only after she has given in, and it arrives as her answer, not the film's pitch. The prompt she types is the premise in plain words, 'Build an app for the songs in my head', and what comes back is a site built around the band that has been chasing her. The line 'Some ideas are too loud to ignore' is held until she is dancing alone, so it sums up something you have just watched rather than setting it up.

## The mix shows where the idea is

The soundtrack is one song, and the film edits its mix the way it edits pictures, to show where the idea is in relation to her. The description says the film was made with the band and presents their sound as the metaphor, but gives no song title, so none is named here. By estimate it runs near 110 BPM (it may be felt at half that). The cuts do not follow the beat: 14 of 49 land within 70 ms of it, about what chance would give. The sync that matters is the mix opening and closing on story points.

On the street the song plays full but without a vocal, rising over the first two seconds. At 9.3 a shop bell rings, a clean tone that hangs for over a second, and the top end of the music falls away, as if the song had been left outside. At about 12.4 the music falls away to little more than a fast, even tick, four to each estimated beat, while the pictures show a hand fidgeting, a boot, drumming fingers. From 15.75 the separated voice track holds a pure, pitched note, and on screen an older man purses his lips as if to whistle. Whether the tick is the song's own hi-hat or sound design, it plays as the shop playing the song. Then an off-screen 'Excuse me' at 17.8 and the bed falls about 20 dB.

Through the cover loop the music comes back muffled, bass and mids with almost nothing above 2 kHz, sinks to a murmur on her face at 22.2, and opens up from 26.3. The sung vocal enters at 27.5, about a second before the singer rises into frame at 28.8, so she hears him first and her head snaps up at 28.1. The vocal stays while he performs, drops out while she walks the street alone (34.5 to 37), and comes back as the cab passes.

The biggest move is at the door. As it swings open on the band, everything above a low rumble disappears for two and a half seconds, and at 44.75 the cut to the wide kitchen and the full mix land within a frame or two of each other, the loudest moment in the film. At 53, with the band gone and her dancing alone, the bass falls away and the song sounds smaller and thinner, roughly as it would from a laptop, which is where it now plays from. The full mix returns a quarter of a second before the end card and fades over the last half second.

## Almost no words

There is no narrator, and the sung words cannot be made out reliably from the separated voice track, so the film's script is a handful of words on screen and a single spoken line. The song works as sound and as a performer, not as lyrics.

The words that are there each do a job. The book cover changes two words, 'Bobby York' to 'Boko Yout', and that swap is the whole joke of the bookshop. The off-screen 'Excuse me' (her lips do not move, so it is presumably the customer) is the only spoken line, and it is used as a hard stop: it wakes her and the music collapses with it.

The prompt is the only sentence the character writes, and it is the film's premise in her own voice: 'Build an app for the songs in my head'. It is typed huge, as the next line of the script rather than as UI. The closing lines, 'Some ideas are too loud to ignore.' and 'For every idea, there's Lovable', are small, plain white sans, and both come after the story has done the work.

## Three rooms, one jacket, and a loop

The film moves through three color worlds: an overcast grey London street, a warm amber bookshop, and a deep green kitchen under a pendant lamp. Her patchwork brown jacket is the constant, so you track her through fast cuts and changes of light. Close-ups are shot shallow, and the shop is told almost entirely in inserts of hands, feet and objects.

The cover stretch is cut as a loop. The same three shots (the cover from above, a tilted shelf, her face) come round three times, the cover alternating between the real book and her version, and her reaction growing each pass: uneasy, wincing, startled. A loop is what an earworm is, and the edit repeats the way the tune does.

The tempo of the cutting follows the pressure. Between 10 and 20 seconds there are fifteen cuts, most under a second. Then the singer's entrance holds for over four seconds, the longest shot so far, and later the slam into the kitchen is cut against two and a half seconds of near stillness. The longest held shots belong to the band and the product, the two things the film wants you to look at.

## The hook planted in the set

The number 925 keeps coming back: as giant '9-2-5' posters with smaller copies stacked like a repeated chorus, as 9:25 on a flip clock in the shop, in raised numerals on her door, and in fridge magnets in the kitchen. The film never explains it. It works as the hook made visible, the same three digits everywhere she looks, and it rewards a second viewing.

Brand color is seeded the same way. The posters are the hot pink of Lovable's gradient, the cartoon worms on the book are pink, and the prompt and end card sit on the full pink, blue and orange glow. The product's world bleeds into the street before the product appears.

The band is real and so is the site. The book cover puts their faces on the worms, the cab and the kitchen give them a London to chase her through, and the site she builds is called 'earworms', plays the band under 'Now playing Boko Yout', and is linked from the description. The demo is something the viewer can go and open, which does more for belief than any claim the film could make.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Mix the music by where the idea is** (see 0:42). When music stands for something, place it in the story with the mix: muffle it when it is in another room, strip it to a pulse when it is in someone's head, bring it back full when they face it, and make it small when it is coming from a device.
- **Slam the sound and the cut on one frame** (see 0:45). Before the big moment, drop the soundtrack to almost nothing for a couple of seconds, then bring the full mix and the widest shot in together, on the same frame if you can.
- **Strip the song to its pulse and let the world play it** (see 0:14). Take the music down to a tick at the song's tempo and cut inserts of hands, feet and objects that seem to keep it. The scene becomes the instrument.
- **Loop the edit, escalate the reaction** (see 0:26). Repeat a short run of shots several times, change one detail each pass, and let only the reaction shot grow. Repetition reads as obsession.
- **Let the voice arrive before the singer** (see 0:28). Bring a vocal in a second before its source appears, so the character turns toward it and the entrance lands as a reveal.
- **Plant the hook in the set dressing** (see 0:05). Repeat one small motif (a number, a color) on posters, a clock, a door and the fridge. Never explain it; let it build up on second viewing.
- **Make the prompt the premise** (see 0:48). Write the product input as the film's metaphor in plain words, and type it large enough to read as the next line of the script.

## Not the lesson here

There is no narration, no feature list, no walkthrough of the interface, and no customers or numbers; the product is on screen for about seven seconds. It is not a film to study for interviews or motion-graphics systems. What it shows is how a song, a mix and a performer's face can carry a product's idea.
