# Craft notes: Arc on Windows. Download now.

The Browser Company · 0:37 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LJQsAOon6og

A thirty-seven-second launch spot built on one joke: the wait. Arc had been Mac-only and then in a long beta on Windows, and instead of hiding that, the film opens in the 1990s, piles up every image of waiting a Windows user remembers (a dial-up picture loading, the hourglass, every Start button from 95 to 11), shows how many people have been in line, and then lets one word, 'Finally', turn the wait into the payoff.

It is worth studying for how much it gets from a timeline split in two. The first fifteen seconds are blurry pixels, nostalgia and a recorded voice reading Marshall McLuhan; the second fifteen are crisp product close-ups with no voice at all. The cut between them is the launch. Then it spends its last three seconds on a joke at Microsoft's expense, dragging Edge into the Recycle Bin.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## A wait, a word, a product

The structure is two halves and a hinge. For fifteen seconds the film is about waiting, and it never mentions Arc: an old PC, a picture of flowers loading a band at a time, 'You've been waiting.', the hourglass, a counter of people in line, and every Start button Windows has had. Then the Arc icon is clicked and one word appears: 'Finally'. For the next fifteen seconds the film is only the product.

The opening works because the wait was real. Arc had been Mac-only, and Windows users had been on a waitlist and then a beta for months. Instead of apologizing, the film turns that into a shared joke and puts the viewer in the line, with 'You've been waiting' addressed to them. The counter makes the joke a fact: 186,142 people onboarded since a date on screen. And the history of the Start button widens the wait from months to decades, so 'Finally' can mean both 'it's out' and 'Windows finally has a browser like this'.

## A borrowed voice, and the film's own words in type

The only voice is a recording, heard over the first eleven seconds: 'The instantaneous world of electric informational media involves all of us, all at once', and 'Ours is a brand-new world of allatonceness.' The words are Marshall McLuhan's, from The Medium Is the Massage (1967); the film does not credit them. It sits in the retro half and reads as archive, part of that world, not as the brand talking. And it is placed to be ironic: 'all at once' plays over an hourglass and a counter of people kept waiting, and 'a brand-new world' plays over thirty years of Start buttons.

Everything the company says itself is in type, and there is very little of it: 'You've been waiting.', 'Finally', 'Arc is on Windows', 'Download now', 'arc.net'. All are white bold sans, centered and small in the frame, one idea per card. The product half has no words at all except the interface's own: the suggestions in the command bar and a tooltip reading 'Open Split View'. The voice stops at 11.4 seconds and never returns, so the product is shown in silence apart from the music.

## Old pixels, then a clean window

The two halves look different before anything is said about them. The past is shot in extreme close-up on soft, enlarged pixels (a title bar's minimize button, IE's Go menu, a caret in a search box, the hourglass), plus a few frames of camcorder footage of a beige PC. The present is a crisp Windows 11 desktop and tight crops of Arc's window, sharp enough to read at phone size. The change of texture at 13.25, from Windows 11's taskbar button to the clean desktop, tells the viewer the history is over.

The Start-button run is the best-made shot in the film. Seven eras of Windows, each in the same framing (the bottom-left corner, the button at the same size, a cursor on it), cut about every half second. Because only the button changes, the cuts read as one continuous click through thirty years, a match cut repeated six times. The same idea, holding the framing and changing the era, is what makes the film's case: the place you click has changed, and now Arc is in it.

## Fifteen seconds of product, one gesture per shot

The product half is about twenty shots in fifteen seconds, and each shows one gesture: typing into the command bar with suggestions, the sidebar sliding in, closing a tab, a link opening in a preview over the page, dragging a tab, dropping one into favorites, opening split view, choosing a material, picking a theme color. None is explained. The film trusts that a viewer who has seen browsers will read each gesture from a second of it, and it frames every shot tight enough that the controls are legible.

Two choices make it feel native to Windows rather than ported. The content is ordinary life (Japandi interiors, an Airbnb in the Hudson Valley, Storm King Art Center, a lo-fi stream, a spicy peanut noodle recipe), not work dashboards, which fits a launch aimed at people who had been waiting to use it. And one shot is a setting only Windows has: 'Mica (default)' or 'Acrylic', the Windows 11 window materials. The montage ends on a soft dissolve through the blurred wallpaper, the one transition that shows the window is translucent.

## The music arrives with the product

One music cue runs from the first frame to the last, with a steady pulse (about 62 BPM by estimate, felt strongly). The edit does not lock to it: only 3 of 35 cuts land within 70 ms of a beat, fewer than chance would give, so the fast cutting follows the gags and gestures, not the beat. Under the voice the music sits at about the voice's level rather than ducking under it, which keeps the McLuhan lines feeling like part of a collage rather than narration.

The level tells the story. The music rises about 5 dB through the Start buttons, dips slightly over the desktop, and jumps about 10 dB right after 'Finally', at 15.5 seconds, as the first Arc window appears. It holds at full level through the whole product half and drops back 10 to 15 dB when the closing cards come up, then fades under the end mark. The release of the wait is heard as well as seen.

## Turning a delay into the pitch

The film persuades with three moves, none of which is a claim about features. It makes the delay a shared joke, so a viewer who was annoyed by the waitlist is now in on it. It shows a number, beta testers onboarded since a stated date, so the launch looks like the end of a queue other people were already in. And it ends with a sting at the browser Windows ships with: Edge dragged to the Recycle Bin, using the system's own 'Move to Recycle Bin' tooltip.

The sting comes after the call to action, not before it, which keeps the cards clean ('Arc is on Windows', 'Download now', 'arc.net') and makes the joke the last thing remembered. The end mark repeats the idea quietly: Arc's 'A' peeking around a Windows logo drawn as a window swung open.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Make the delay the joke** (see 0:04). If people have been waiting for your launch, open on the wait. Use images they remember waiting on, address them directly ('You've been waiting.'), then release it with one word on the cut to the product.
- **Count the line** (see 0:07). Show a real, rising number of people who already signed up, with the date it counts from, so the launch reads as the end of a queue and not a cold start.
- **Repeat a framing across eras** (see 0:10). Shoot the same small piece of an interface from successive versions in an identical crop, with the cursor in the same spot, and cut every half second. Only the era changes, so history plays like one gesture.
- **Change texture at the turn** (see 0:13). Shoot the past soft and pixelated and the present crisp. The viewer feels the move to now before anything says it.
- **One gesture per product shot** (see 0:22). Give each feature one tight shot of one action (type, drag, drop, open) and no caption. Let the interface's own tooltip name the one feature that needs a name.
- **Show the platform's own settings** (see 0:27). To prove a port is native, include one setting only that platform has.
- **Put the sting after the call to action** (see 0:34). End the cards cleanly, then add a short gag at a competitor's expense, using the system's own UI, so the joke is the last thing remembered.

## Not the lesson here

No one from the company speaks or appears, nothing is explained, there are no performance claims, prices or comparisons beyond the closing gag, and the McLuhan recording is not credited. Do not study it for explaining a product; study it for structure, timing and how a launch can turn its own delay into the story.
