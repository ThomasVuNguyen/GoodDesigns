# Craft notes: Meet Custom Agents: Your 24/7 AI Team

Notion · 1:11 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yrEic418kjg

A seventy-one second launch film with no voice and almost no cuts. It opens on a conveyor belt of chores that piles into a heap of 628 to-dos and blows away. Then it introduces three AI agents by turning their sidebar icons into characters: a purple checkbox, an orange speech bubble and a teal folder, each with two dot eyes. Each one gets a title and a short demo on real Notion screens. Then the film goes to night and pulls back to show the agents working by the dozen while nobody is watching.

What is worth learning is how little it invents. The characters are the app's own icons, the busywork is written in the app's own checkbox type, and the demos are real views (a board, a thread, a database) shown in a few seconds each. One repeated entrance, the badge sweeping its title on, turns three features into one system. A hand that flicks a light bulb carries the film from day to night.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## A problem you can count, then three jobs

The structure is simple: show the problem, wave it goodbye, meet the helpers, watch each one work, then show what happens when nobody is watching. The problem gets twelve seconds, which is a lot in a seventy-second film, and it is made physical. Tasks ride a conveyor, more keep landing, the belt runs out and they pile up. Then a purple 'To-do' pill puts a number on the heap, 628, so the feeling of too much becomes a figure.

The middle is a list of three, and each item has the same shape: a title, a starting state, the agent doing something, an end state. Requests go from an inbox to a prioritized board. Questions go from 43 unread to 0. A database gets this week's report, and readers' avatars stack up beside it. Each demo ends on a result, not on a feature.

The ending raises the stakes without a new claim. 'Keep work moving all day...' is completed by '...and night.' across a cut to navy, and the camera steps back twice until the three agents are hundreds. The last image before the logo is a button being pressed: the film ends on the viewer making their own.

- 0:41 `seconds/second-00042.webp` The unread count reaches 0: the Q&A demo ends on a result.
- 1:02 `seconds/second-00063.webp` Stepped back twice, the agents are small marks on a grid. Scale is the last argument.
- 1:04 `seconds/second-00065.webp` The final beat is a button pressed, not a slogan.

## Nine lines, and a button

Nobody speaks. The script is written on screen in short lines of bold black sans, centered, and each is on screen for one or two seconds. They come in pairs and sets. 'Bye bye, busywork.' is answered by 'Hello, Custom Agents.' The three capability lines share a grammar: a verb, an object and an adverb ('Route tasks automatically', 'Answer questions instantly', 'Write updates autonomously').

The best line is split across a cut. 'Keep work moving all day...' ends on an ellipsis, and '...and night.' picks it up on navy, so the punctuation does the joining. The last words in the film are not a slogan but a button label, '+ New Agent', which a cursor presses.

Everything else is said by demo data, and it is written to sound like a real office: 'Where's the demo script?', 'Is the .heic fix a top priority?', 'What's the wifi password?'. Those questions explain what a Q&A agent is for faster than a line of copy could.

- 0:37 `seconds/second-00038.webp` The demo questions are the copy: the wifi password, a conference room.
- 0:54 `seconds/second-00055.webp` '...and night.' finishes the line from the previous shot.

## The sidebar icons become the cast

The film builds its world from things already in the product. At 0:16 three agents appear in Notion's real sidebar with small round icons: a purple checkbox, an orange speech bubble, a teal folder. A second later those same icons are characters, drawn bigger with a thick black outline and two dot eyes, and they stay the cast for the rest of the film. Each agent's color then runs through its own section: purple speed lines and title for routing, orange for questions, teal for updates.

Around them is a small, consistent kit. Warm off-white for day and deep navy for night. Thick black hand-drawn line for the conveyor belt and the outlines. Halftone-stippled hands, like a printed cartoon, that place the badges and later flick the light. Real Notion UI laid flat on the same paper with no device frame and no shadow, so the drawn world and the product share one surface.

The busywork is written in the product's own type too. The task cards use Notion's checkbox and font, so the opening heap is already a Notion view before the 'To-do' pill confirms it. At night the agents glow and drag their tasks behind them like banners, and the film quietly adds new characters (a light bulb, a plug and more) to suggest there are many more kinds.

## Each demo is one before-and-after

The product moments are short (about eight seconds each) and each shows one change of state. The UI is real and readable but cropped to the part that matters: a column of message cards, a board, a thread, a database row. The agent never clicks a menu. It flies in and physically moves the work, carrying a stack of requests to To-do, lifting the Urgent card to the top, dropping a new row into a table.

Counters do the explaining. The heap's count climbs to 628, the questions' unread badge runs up to 43 and back down to 0, and the status page's readers stack up to '+3'. A number changing is understood at a glance, which matters when nobody is speaking.

The data is specific enough to feel lived in: named people with hand-drawn avatars, priority tags like 'Urgent' for Data Governance, 'New launch date is April 14. Here's the rollout plan.' as a real answer to a real question. It is also legible in a second, which is all it gets.

## Moving without cutting

There are only about a dozen hard cuts in the film, and most scene changes happen inside the motion. The heap bursts to reveal its own title. A badge sweeps through at huge scale and leaves a line of text in its wake. Messages are carried off one view as the next view slides in. The eye always has something to follow from one idea to the next.

The few real cuts are placed with care. The cut to the product lands on the beat drop. The cut to night happens in the middle of a flick: the bulb keeps its arc and the hand finishes its follow-through on navy, so the move carries straight over a total change of color. The night pull-back is done in two blinks to empty navy rather than a zoom, so each step out lands as a jump in scale.

Composition stays centered and open. Titles sit alone in the middle of the frame with a lot of empty paper around them, and the UI is cropped so one column or one thread fills the space. Nothing competes with the badge.

- 0:33 `seconds/second-00034.webp` The orange badge drags its title on: a transition and a title in one move.
- 0:54 `seconds/second-00055.webp` The flick carries across the cut to night, the bulb still in flight.

## A held breath before the product

There is no voice and the soundtrack is one music cue; the stems show no separate effects worth naming. It is shaped around three moments. It starts near silent with only light ticks under the belt, adds low stabs in blocks, then thickens and rises while the count climbs and the heap bursts. Then, under 'Bye bye, busywork.', the mix falls to nearly nothing for about a second and a half (from 13.3 to 14.8). The full beat drops at 14.8, a tenth of a second before the cut to the product sidebar.

From there it is a steady groove, near 77 BPM by estimate (an estimate that can be double or half the felt tempo). The two cuts the detector found do not land on its beat, so it works as a bed, not a metronome. A brief voice-like chop at about 20 seconds falls just as the badges dash off; the separator files it as voice, but there are no clear words. Short pitched figures join from about 51 seconds and carry through the night.

The ending is placed exactly. The groove stops at about 65 seconds, just after the cursor presses '+ New Agent' and right as the logo appears. A soft tail without the beat carries the wordmark, and it ends at 68.4, leaving the last two and a half seconds silent.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Cast the product's own icons** (see 0:18). Show the icons in the real UI first, then enlarge them, outline them and give them eyes. The characters then point straight back to the product, and each one's color can mark its section.
- **Make the problem a pile with a number** (see 0:11). Show the pain as a physical heap that keeps growing, then attach the product's own counter to it. Viewers feel the mess and read the scale at once.
- **Give every feature the same entrance** (see 0:23). Introduce each capability with an identical move (here the badge sweeping its title on), changing only the color. Repetition makes a list of features read as one system and tells the viewer a new chapter has started.
- **End each demo on a count** (see 0:41). Let a number that goes up or down carry the result: 43 unread to 0, readers stacking to '+3'. With no voice, the counter is the narration.
- **Drop the music before the product** (see 0:15). Pull the cue almost to silence for a second or two before the first product shot, and bring the beat back on the cut. The product arrives as a release.
- **Split a line across a cut** (see 0:54). End the first half on an ellipsis and open the next shot with one, and carry a moving object through the cut. Here a flicked light bulb turns 'all day...' into '...and night.'
- **Step back to show scale** (see 1:02). After showing one of each, cut back twice to wider views until the individuals become a pattern. It makes the 'all the time, everywhere' claim without saying it.
- **End on the button** (see 1:04). Make the call to action a real UI control that a cursor presses, then go to the logo. The viewer sees the first step instead of reading an instruction.

## Not the lesson here

There are no people on camera, no voiceover, no customers and no numbers about the product itself. The film also skips setup entirely (the triggers and schedules the description mentions are never shown), so it is not a guide to explaining configuration. Study it for building a small motion world out of a product's own UI.
