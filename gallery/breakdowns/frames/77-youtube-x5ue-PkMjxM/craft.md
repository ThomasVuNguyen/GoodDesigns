# Craft notes: Why you should never vote: an explainer

Jon Bois · 2:37 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x5ue-PkMjxM

A two-and-a-half-minute parody of the explainer video, posted on May 4, 2016. It looks like one: a numbered list of six reasons, a calm narrator, a map with numbers on it, a scale comparison, a form to download, a pie chart. Every one of them is broken, and the narrator never notices. The case against voting gets less coherent with each reason, from a voter fee that does not exist to 'Plus voter fraud with all of the fraud', and the pictures go from tidy cards to a night-vision corridor to a phone pointed at a laptop.

It is worth studying for two things. One is the deadpan: the voice keeps one even, reasonable tone from start to finish, so all of the escalation is carried by the script and the pictures, and the gap between them is the joke. The other is how cheaply it is built: cut-out photos, clip art, government forms and old interface screenshots pasted on one mint canvas, tinted to a single green, with a virtual camera doing almost all of the motion and only 11 hard cuts.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## Six reasons, each worse than the last

The structure is the most familiar one on YouTube: a question ('have you ever wondered what makes voting such an awful thing to do?'), then a numbered list. The parody is in how the list decays. Reason one is a false fact delivered with evidence: a fee map and a scale comparison. Reason two is a real complaint, voting on a Tuesday during work hours, with a calendar. Reason three starts as a bureaucratic obstacle and keeps going for 48 seconds, a third of the film, through a URL, a printer, an error dialog, a System Administrator's address, the census and a hallway, until it ends on 'They will all see you.' After that the reasons are a feeling (people will think you are strange), an insult (all of the candidates are bad, especially this one) and arithmetic that does not work.

The last reason does not end, it dissolves: 'Plus the electoral stuff, so who cares. And plus all of the government problems with the money debt... Plus voter fraud with all of the fraud. Think about it.' The pictures dissolve with it, from the canvas to a phone filming search queries. The film never says to vote. It lets the case against voting sound the way a bad argument sounds, and leaves the conclusion to the viewer.

## A reasonable voice saying unreasonable things

The narrator sounds like an explainer narrator: clear, even and never in a hurry. The level never changes for a joke. 'That's a lot of money' gets the same reading as 'Filling it out incorrectly exposes you to federal prosecution' and 'They will all see you.' Because the voice does not escalate, the script and the pictures have to, and the viewer is the only one who notices.

The script borrows explainer phrasing and empties it. 'Picture it like this' sets up a comparison that compares nothing ('If you have a lot more money than that, then you have $1 million'). 'Mathematically speaking' introduces 1,000 voters and 1%. The sentences stay grammatical and confident while their content falls apart, until the last ones lose their grammar too: 'the government problems with the money debt'. The words on screen add their own errors that the voice never mentions: the chart reads '10,000 Americans', and the fee map is for a '2014 Presidential election'.

- 0:21 `seconds/second-00022.webp` 'Picture it like this': a line of bills with nothing to compare it to.
- 1:06 `seconds/second-00067.webp` 'Filling it out incorrectly exposes you to federal prosecution,' read like a weather report.
- 2:07 `seconds/second-00128.webp` 'So that means your vote only counts for 1% of the overall vote.'
- 2:13 `seconds/second-00134.webp` 'Plus all of the government problems with the money debt.'

## One mint canvas, cut-outs and real paperwork

The look is built from found pieces under one rule. Every prop is a cut-out photo (gems, a cinder block, a shovel, a fork, an electrical outlet, a crowbar, candidates' faces) tinted into the same dark green, and every card uses the same layout: a numeral, a bold geometric sans for the claim, an italic serif for captions and dollar figures, hairline rules. The rule is broken on purpose by the things that are meant to feel foreign: Form Q-83 stays stark white, the Internet Explorer 6 ad and the street map keep their own colors, the Windows dialog stays grey. The bureaucracy looks like it does not belong on the page.

The paperwork is real or close to it: a procurement form with lines for DUNS numbers and markup rates, the 2010 Census form, a Windows printers folder, an 'IBM Rational Portfolio Manager' database error. Real artifacts make the frustration recognizable faster than drawn ones would. The live footage is treated to feel hostile. The corridor is a panel pasted on the canvas and tinted night-vision green, and ordinary voters standing in line are cut in as an inverted blue negative, which makes a meeting room look like surveillance.

- 0:43 `seconds/second-00044.webp` Form Q-83, left white so it looks out of place.
- 1:11 `seconds/second-00072.webp` The corridor, tinted night vision, with a lower third out of a conspiracy theory.

## A camera travelling across one page

The film has 11 detected hard cuts in 157 seconds, and the first does not come until 0:46.7. Everything before it is camera movement over a single surface. Each reason card has its props laid out below or beside it, and the camera tilts down to them, pushes into a detail, then whips sideways to the next card. The layout shows through twice: at 0:06.75 the card for reason one and the map sit side by side, small, and at 1:02 a half-second whip back up the canvas runs past every piece of reason three in rows before landing on Form Q-83.

The moves change character as the argument does. Reasons one and two get smooth, centered tilts and pans. Reason three whips past an ad, scans a URL, spins into a map and cuts hard across the form's fine print. The corridor is entered by panning onto its rectangle and left the same way, so even it belongs to the page. The cuts gather where the film loses its footing: four take the inverted voters in and out (1:31.7 to 1:44.5), and three carry the ending, from the pie chart to a phone filming a laptop at 2:15.7 and on to the end card. Leaving the canvas for that phone is the sign that the argument has collapsed.

- 0:55 `seconds/second-00056.webp` The map spins as it zooms, the most disorienting move so far.
- 1:02 `seconds/second-00063.webp` Halfway through the whip back up the canvas.

## Jokes for whoever pauses

Much of the comedy is in the fine print, too small or too brief to catch at speed. The fee map includes Cuba, at $68. The calendar is headed 'welcome to this calendar' and has a '?' between Sunday and Monday. The Department of Registrars was established in 1247 and has an all-seeing eye and a square and compass in its logo. An Internet Explorer 6 ad flashes by in a whip pan. The candidates' faces carry a leftover production note, 'NOTE TO JON: remember to take these guys out, they aren't in the race anymore. they were too handsome. love, jon', pointing at Cruz and Kasich, who dropped out the day before and the day of the upload.

The catalog boxes do the same work at every reason: 'Voting stones', 'Voting tea', 'The Incredible Voting Dog' (a cinder block), 'Voter's Helper™' (a shovel), 'Voter's supper' (a fork and a power outlet), 'Emergency voting kit' (a Dracula DVD and a crowbar). The voice never mentions them. They make the repeated template a reason to look at each card, and the end card keeps it up: 'Please do not share this video with anyone. Sharing is a form of theft.'

## One cue, never in on the joke

There is one music cue for the whole film, at about 129 BPM with a steady pulse, and it sits a few decibels under the voice (between 2 and 9 dB in most ten-second windows, 11 dB at the end) without obvious ducking. It does not change for the corridor, the faces or the pie chart, so, like the voice, it keeps a pleasant explainer surface over the escalation. The cuts are not timed to it: 3 of the 11 land within 70 ms of a beat, about what chance would give. The one change comes when the voice stops at 2:24: the cue rises to its full level under the last search and the end card.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Keep the voice flat and escalate the pictures** (see 1:24). Read the absurd lines in the same even tone as the sensible ones, and let the script and images get stranger. The audience supplies the reaction the narrator never has.
- **Break the explainer's own tools** (see 2:07). Use the familiar devices (a data map, a scale comparison, a pie chart, a numbered list) and get each one subtly wrong: a year with no election, a comparison to nothing, a 1% slice that is a sliver. The parody works because the form is right.
- **Lay the film out on one canvas** (see 0:06). Paste every card, prop and footage panel onto one large surface and move a virtual camera over it with tilts, whips, pushes and pulls instead of cuts. Let the layout show for a moment, so viewers feel the space.
- **Plant the next prop early** (see 0:12). Put the object the next line needs in the corner of the current shot, like the dollar bill beside the fee map, then move the camera to it when the voice gets there.
- **Fix the template, vary the joke** (see 1:45). Give every list item the identical layout, so the only thing that changes is the absurd part: here the caption and product in each catalog box.
- **Degrade the production with the argument** (see 2:16). Let the craft fall apart as the reasoning does: from tidy cards to tinted footage to a phone filming a laptop. The picture quality becomes part of the script.
- **Hide jokes in the fine print** (see 1:11). Put lines too small or brief to catch at speed (an establishment date, a leftover note, an ad in a whip pan) so the film rewards pausing and rewatching.

## Not the lesson here

Nothing in it is accurate, and it is not meant to be: there is no fee to vote, no Form Q-83, no presidential election in 2014, and nothing about how voting actually works. There is no on-camera host and no sources, and the soundtrack is one cue under one voice. Study it for deadpan timing, for building a parody out of an explainer's own tools, and for making a cheap, coherent world on one canvas with a moving camera.
