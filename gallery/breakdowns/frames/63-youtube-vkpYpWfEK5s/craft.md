# Craft notes: Think Together

Notion · 2:05 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vkpYpWfEK5s

A brand manifesto that argues one sentence for two minutes: the most meaningful things were never built alone. It answers the story that AI is one founder and a chat window, and it does it almost entirely with found pictures, a single narrator and a few words on black. The product appears once, for four seconds.

What makes it worth studying is how the images are chosen and placed to argue. The past is a scrapbook of groups (a five-man bicycle, a studio staff photo, a campfire, a big band), laid out as prints on a black page and paired so each pair makes a point. The lonely future is told in headlines, a terminal and empty offices shot on a phone. Then the layout itself starts to act out the lines: a clip clones itself for 'ten times faster', a window widens for 'more minds', a call grid fills for 'not behind one person's screen', and the present-day people finally fill the whole frame.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## A thesis, its evidence, its opponent

The film is built like an essay. It states its claim early ('The most meaningful things were never built alone'), proves it with history, lets the other side speak ('Billion-dollar companies with a solo founder behind a chat window'), turns ('We think that gets the future wrong'), offers its own picture of the future, and only then says where Notion stands. The narrator does not name the company until 1:16, more than halfway through.

The order of the evidence matters. It starts with achievements everyone knows (the moon landing, the Disney studio, the first flights) and then slides toward the ordinary: two men at a radio, women on a break, a campfire, friends at a chess board. By the time it reaches 'Togetherness gives us meaning', the film has moved from what groups build to why people want to be together at all.

The ending is the thesis turned around. The opening said great things were never built alone; the close says 'The best things in humanity haven't been built yet. They will be built by us together.' It is the same sentence, pointed forward, over children climbing a sculpture.

- 0:11 `seconds/second-00012.webp` The claim is stated in the first fifteen seconds, one word at a time.
- 0:24 `seconds/second-00025.webp` The evidence drifts from landmarks to ordinary afternoons.
- 0:45 `seconds/second-00046.webp` The opposing story gets its own sequence, told fairly enough to recognize.
- 1:54 `seconds/second-00115.webp` The close restates the thesis as something still to come.

## One narrator, four registers of text

There is only one borrowed voice, and it comes first: the Apollo landing call, 'Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.' It is short, instantly recognized, and it lets history speak before the brand does. Everything after it is one narrator, speaking in short, plain phrases with room around them. A man is seen talking into a microphone at 1:27, but we never hear him; the film keeps a single voice so the argument stays one person's conviction.

The words on screen are split into registers. Burned-in yellow captions carry every narrated phrase, and the archive line is set in italics so you can tell it apart. The five lines that carry the argument (the thesis, the turn, 'abundance', 'more opportunities' and the closing line) drop the captions and the pictures entirely and appear one word per card, white on black, timed to the voice. The opposing view is never spoken by anyone else. It arrives as real headlines, each in its own publication's typeface, so the 'loudest story' is literally the press, quoted.

The customers are described rather than named. The narrator says 'The fintech startup in New York with more ambition than people' and 'The three founders in Lagos who don't know yet what they're about to become', while a small 'RAMP' or 'HEIDI' sits in the corner of the frame for anyone who looks. The line describes a situation the viewer might share; the logo is a footnote.

- 0:13 `seconds/second-00014.webp` A thesis line gets no caption and no picture, only the word being spoken.
- 0:41 `seconds/second-00042.webp` The counter-argument is quoted from the press, in the papers' own type.
- 1:40 `seconds/second-00101.webp` Spoken as 'the fintech startup in New York', credited small in the corner.

## Pictures chosen in pairs, placed on a black page

Most of the archive is laid on black like prints on a table, and the most important images come in pairs. The astronaut stands next to the room that got him there. A pencil Mickey sits next to the finished cartoon on 'until something clicks'. A cave painting waits alone in half the frame until a campfire joins it. Eight men under a company logo sit beside a couple at a laboratory bench. Each pair makes a small argument that neither picture makes alone.

Size carries meaning too. The past and the lonely present are mostly small prints with black around them: the early computer lab, the terminal, the empty-office phone clip. The present-day teams, from 1:12 on, fill the frame edge to edge. The change is announced by the mission control shot at 1:08, which opens from a slit to full bleed on 'More minds on any problem'. After that, people take up the whole screen.

The choice of images is generous and specific. Alongside landmarks there are puppets, a big band laughing, a dog at a desk, friends doing thumbs up at a camera. The lonely images, by contrast, are drained of people and of craft: an unmade bed in a dark apartment, empty desks, a shaky vertical phone clip. The film never shows a villain. It shows rooms where people used to be.

Black is used as punctuation. A second and a half of it follows the moon landing, nearly two seconds follow the turn, and short black beats separate each of the three customers, so every story gets its own breath.

## Layouts that act out the line

There are no animated graphics, but the layout performs. When the narrator says 'ten times faster doesn't make a company ten times better', one factory clip doubles and then fills the frame as four identical copies working in lockstep: more output, the same four people. When the line is 'More minds on any problem', the frame opens in three steps to let more heads into mission control. On 'Not behind one person's screen', a call grid fills tile by tile until six faces share the screen.

The same trick is used for small jokes. A flat digital chessboard pops up between two friends exactly on 'even though machines do it better'. The solo founder's terminal types a prompt asking for an app that is 'basically Salesforce meets Notion meets Slack', a dry nod for anyone who pauses on it. A trading-floor photo is boxed in by live-looking ticker numbers for 'Not just the Fortune 500'.

All of these are hard pops between states, never smooth tweens, which keeps them in the same plain, editorial voice as the cutting. The rule is easy to borrow: decide what a line claims, then let the arrangement of the picture make the same claim.

- 1:25 `seconds/second-00086.webp` Six people fill 'one person's screen'.
- 0:30 `seconds/second-00031.webp` A machine's chessboard set between two laughing players.

## Music that follows the argument, not the cuts

The soundtrack is one narrator over one score, and the score changes section by section with the essay. The opening, under the Apollo call and the thesis, is a low drone; two short high beeps bracket the landing line like mission radio tones, and a few electronic chirps sit under the early computer pictures. The music proper, a pitched pulsing cue, starts at 15.4, on the cut to the five-man bicycle, as the proof begins.

At the turn it steps back. Around 53.3 the cue drops to a drone again under 'future wrong' and the held black, and at 55.7, on the cut to the factory switch, it returns louder and fuller and carries the rest of the film. By the stems, the music sits about 9 to 15 dB under the voice throughout, and no hits are placed on cuts: the tempo estimate is near 123 BPM (which may be double or half the felt pulse), and cuts land on the beat no more often than chance. The pictures are cut to the sentences instead.

The best decision is at the end. The music fades out at about 116, after 'haven't been built yet', so 'They will be built by us together' is spoken with almost nothing under it. A warm low chord comes back only with the end card at 121. The silence makes the last line sound like a person talking, not a voiceover.

## Warmth from ordinary, unpolished people

For a film about AI it has almost no technology in it, and that is part of why it feels sincere. The present-day footage is candid and a little rough: selfies at events, a slightly blown-out thumbs-up, a woman waving with her dog beside her, people laughing at a laptop. These read as real teams rather than casting, and the customers are shown as groups, from three founders on a sofa to a staff photo of a hundred or more.

The script also admits something. 'About a year ago, we took a hard look at where AI was headed. The future felt lonely and unsettling.' A brand confessing that it was unsettled, and quoting real headlines rather than inventing a strawman, earns more trust than a list of features would. The product gets four seconds on a video call, and the film ends on 'us' held a beat longer than any other word.

- 1:14 `seconds/second-00075.webp` A wave and a dog: footage that feels taken, not staged.
- 0:37 `seconds/second-00038.webp` Real headlines stand in for the opposing view.
- 1:44 `seconds/second-00105.webp` A customer shown as a whole team, not a spokesperson.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Let an archive voice speak first** (see 0:02). Open on a short, famous recording before your narrator says a word. It sets the scale of the claim and borrows history's authority for a few seconds.
- **Argue in pairs** (see 0:21). Put two pictures side by side so that together they make the point: the lone hero and the room behind him, the sketch and the finished thing, the old ritual and its modern form.
- **Give the thesis its own register** (see 0:11). Choose the four or five lines that carry the argument and show them one word per card on black, timed to the voice, with no picture and no caption. Everything else stays captioned.
- **Quote the opposing story** (see 0:41). Show the view you are arguing against as real headlines in their own typefaces, piling up on an empty page, instead of putting words in an imaginary opponent's mouth.
- **Make the layout say the line** (see 1:08). If the line is about multiplying, multiply the clip; if it is about more people, open the frame to let more in; if it is about one screen, fill that screen with faces.
- **Shrink the lonely, fill the frame with the together** (see 1:13). Keep isolation and the past small on black, and let the people you are arguing for take the full frame once the turn has happened.
- **Describe customers, credit them small** (see 1:40). Have the narrator name a customer by its situation ('the three founders in Lagos') and put the logo in a corner, so the viewer recognizes themselves before the brand.
- **Pull the music for the last line** (see 1:59). Fade the score just before the closing sentence and bring a single chord back on the end card, so the final words land as speech.

## Not the lesson here

There is no product demo, no feature list, no numbers and no interviews; the one person seen speaking is never heard. Nothing is animated beyond hard changes of layout, and the music is a bed, not a beat to cut to. Study it for how found images, one voice and a few words on black are sequenced into an argument.
