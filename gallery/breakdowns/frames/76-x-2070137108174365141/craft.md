# Craft notes: Best-performing @browsercompany feature of all time:

Josh Miller (@joshm) · 0:30 · https://x.com/joshm/status/2070137108174365141

A thirty-second comparison ad in which nobody says a word. The same woman sits at the same desk and opens the same laptop twice. 'Mornings with Chrome.' turns into a scream that spreads to nine strangers; 'Mornings with Dia.' turns into a painting, a calm look and a brief that tells her what to do first. The Chrome screen is never shown. You know what is on it from the sound.

It is worth studying almost entirely for its sound design. The screams are silent; a siren and a climbing electronic cue scream for them. The turn between the two halves is a hit and a second of digital silence. The calm morning starts on birdsong alone, and the product reveal is timed to a bird's coo that seems to come out of the painting on screen. Take the sound away and the film is two nearly identical takes; with it, it is a story with a villain and a relief.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## The soundtrack tells the story

The Chrome half is built as one long crescendo. It starts on plain room sound (the chair, a thump as the film cuts to the laptop), then a bright, chiming cue under the title. Notification sounds pile in as she reads, a ding and a run of plops and chimes between 2.0 and 3.5, so the screen we never see is heard as a stream of interruptions. The level climbs without a break, from about -40 dBFS at the title to -13 dBFS at the peak, roughly 27 dB in ten seconds. A sound-event tagger hears the second half of the climb as tense, electronic music.

Her scream is never heard. The isolated voice stem is empty through the whole first half, and on the cut to her close-up at 5.58 a siren arrives in its place. The nine strangers scream silently too. Leaving the voices out keeps the joke from turning ugly (ten real screams would be hard to sit through) and lets the music scream for everyone at once, which reads as a feeling rather than a noise.

The turn is the cleanest edit in the film. On the cut to black at 9.92 a single hit lands, and a quarter second later the soundtrack is digital silence. After ten seconds of build, the silence is felt as relief before any picture explains it. Then birdsong fades up from nothing with the same wide shot, very quiet (around -57 dBFS), and the film holds on birds and a calm face for two seconds before the strings enter with 'Mornings with Dia.'.

The Dia half is not one even bed of music. The strings play in phrases that ease off on each beat of the story: they thin almost to silence over the painting on the wall, dip for half a second before the laptop's title appears, and dip again on the cut to the end card. Into one of those gaps the film puts a bird's coo, exactly as 'The Monday Brief' fades up over the painting on screen, so the product's first frame seems to have a sound of its own. A whoosh carries the camera into the screen, and soft clicks mark the press of 'Prep for this'. The cutting follows the story, not a beat: only 4 of 15 detected cuts land within 70 ms of the music's estimated beat, fewer than chance.

## Two takes of one morning

The structure is a before and after shot as a repeat. The same woman, the same green shirt, the same desk and the same locked-off wide are used for both mornings, and the two titles are the same sentence with one word changed: 'Mornings with Chrome.', 'Mornings with Dia.'. Because nothing in the frame changes, the viewer compares only what does: her face, the pace of the cutting and the sound.

The first half spends ten seconds turning a small thing (opening a laptop) into a collective scream, with strangers on black making it everyone's morning. The second half spends ten seconds doing almost nothing: she looks at a painting, smiles, opens the laptop. The product only arrives in the last third, and by then the viewer wants it.

## Holding the shot, then matching it

The camera barely moves, which makes the cuts do the work. In the Chrome half the shots shrink from nearly three seconds to a quarter of a second, ending in a whip pan across her shirt and a hard cut to black. The strangers are framed the same way, head-on, on black, mouths open at the lens, so the cuts between them read as one scream passed along a line.

The Dia half moves by matching. Her eyeline leads to the painting on the wall; the painting comes back on the laptop when the lid opens; the brief then floats over a painted landscape in the same style. Each shot hands its image to the next, so the product looks like it grew out of her room. The two lid openings rhyme as well: the first is a soft, out-of-focus insert, and the second is a sharp low angle that shows the screen.

## Five seconds of product, all of it content

Dia gets about five seconds of screen time, and it shows one feature, the Monday Brief, as a finished thing rather than a demo. There is no typing and no prompt. The page opens on her painting and the date, lists her to-dos with the context behind each ('He needs a clear yes/no…'), and ends on 'You have a busy day today!' with a yellow starburst to prep for a meeting. The footer names where it all came from: Google Calendar, Slack, Notion and GitHub.

The brief is designed to look like part of her room, not like software. It uses her painting as its header, a yellow serif title, and a hand-cut sticker as its button, and it floats over a painted backdrop instead of a desktop. The Chrome screen, by contrast, is never shown at all; the film trusts the viewer to fill it in with their own inbox.

## Felt before it is explained

The film names its competitor in the first title and never says anything bad about it. It does not need to: the viewer hears ten seconds of pings, a siren and a rising cue, and then silence, and takes the side of the silence before any product appears. The claim is made in the body, through the change in sound, which is harder to argue with than a line of copy.

The only argument in words comes at the very end, and it is modest: 'A better way to start your work day.' By then the film has already shown the better way as a feeling (birds, a painting, a smile) and then as a page. Josh Miller's post calls it the company's best-performing feature video, which suggests the restraint did not cost it reach.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Let the score scream instead of the actors** (see 0:06). For an extreme reaction, cut the human voice and give its sound to the music and an effect (here a siren). It keeps the feeling and loses the noise, and it lets one sound stand for many people.
- **Build, hit, silence** (see 0:10). Run one uninterrupted crescendo, end it on a single hit over a cut to black, and drop to true silence for most of a second. The silence is felt as relief before the next shot explains it.
- **Reshoot the same frame with opposite sound** (see 0:11). For a before and after, keep the actor, the set and the camera position identical and change only the sound and the pace of the cutting. The viewer compares the feeling, not the props.
- **Plant the product's first image in the room** (see 0:18). Show an object in the set (here a painting) with an eyeline cut, and make it the first thing on screen when the product opens. The product looks like it belongs to the person.
- **Give the reveal a sound from inside the picture** (see 0:20). Clear a half-second gap in the music and fill it with a sound that belongs to the image on screen (a bird's coo over a landscape painting) as the product's title appears.
- **Change one word in the title card** (see 0:13). Use the same sentence, type and position for both halves and change only the name. The repetition makes the comparison without a word of criticism.

## Not the lesson here

There is no voice, no narration, no on-screen explanation of how the Monday Brief is made, and no shot of Chrome itself. The product is shown for about five seconds, as a finished page. Do not study it for explaining a feature; study it for how sound, silence and a repeated shot can carry a comparison on their own.
