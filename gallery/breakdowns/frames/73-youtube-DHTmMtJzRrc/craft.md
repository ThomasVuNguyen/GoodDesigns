# Craft notes: Tidy Tabs by Arc | A browser that tidies for you

The Browser Company · 0:21 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DHTmMtJzRrc

A twenty-one second ad for one feature. Tidy Tabs is a button at the top of Arc's sidebar that sorts your open tabs into labeled groups. The film has no voice and nine words of copy, set one or two at a time on Arc's blue: 'Don't worry', 'Tab freely', 'Tidy Tabs', 'Now available in Arc'. Everything else is the sidebar itself, magnified until a tab row is as big as a headline.

What is worth learning is how it demonstrates a feature that takes a moment to run. The first demo is one take: tabs pile up, a click, the divider ripples while it works, the camera pulls back, and the tabs fall into groups. After that the film shows the feature three more times in other people's Spaces, and it cuts each repeat down to just the click or just the sort. The viewer learns the whole thing once and then sees proof that it works on anyone's mess.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## The answer comes before the problem

The film opens on 'Don't worry' before there is anything to worry about. Only then do the tabs start piling up, and the next card is 'Tab freely'. The usual order for a feature ad is problem, then solution. Here the comfort comes first, so the mess that follows reads as harmless: you are being told to go ahead and make it. The YouTube description says the missing half out loud ('Going down a rabbit hole? Don't worry, tab freely'), but the film leaves the rabbit hole to the picture.

The name comes third. 'Tidy Tabs' appears only after the viewer has watched it work once, so the name labels something already understood instead of introducing something to be explained. 'Now available in Arc' and 'arc.net' close it out. There is no description of how it works, no mention of AI, and no benefit stated in words beyond 'tab freely'.

## One full demo, then three fast repeats

The first demonstration runs for three seconds without a cut. It starts close on 'Clear' and its broom icon, the pointer presses it, and the divider beside it turns into a moving wavy line, Arc's sign that the feature is working. While the line is still wavy, the view pulls back so the whole unsorted list is visible next to the page. Then the tabs rearrange into 'Vacuum Shopping', 'Movies' and 'San Diego Zoo'. Because the shot never cuts, the viewer can trust that this is what the button does, wait included.

The repeats are edited down. The salmon Space gets the close-up and the wavy line, then cuts to the wide shot just before the sort. The teal and orange Spaces get no click at all, only a wide shot that starts messy and is sorted within half a second. By then the viewer knows what caused it. Each repeat is about 1.2 to 1.4 seconds, so in five seconds the film shows three more sorts without asking the viewer to wait through the feature again.

The interface is shown huge. In the close-ups a tab title is set as large as the title cards, and even the wide shots show only the sidebar and a slice of the page. The rest of the browser, the address bar and the window chrome never appear. The film shows the one part of the product the feature touches and enlarges it until it is legible on a phone.

## Tabs from that week

The film came out on 7 February 2024, and the tabs belong to that week: Super Bowl 58 predictions, Valentine's Day lists, the Grammys, a Vision Pro durability test a few days after the headset shipped, Oppenheimer during awards season. Each Space is a plausible person on a plausible day, which does more than generic placeholder tabs would to make the mess feel like the viewer's own.

Each Space also has its own color and wallpaper (sage over a pine forest, salmon over a misty mountain, teal over a painted sky, orange over a dark forest), which is how Arc users really tell their Spaces apart. The colors make the repeats read as different people at a glance, and they rhyme with the flat blue cards, so the whole film looks like one palette. One of the sorted groups is 'Arc Browser', with Arc's own launch video in it.

## Cards and screens, cut in turns

The film alternates strictly between two kinds of shot: a flat blue card with one or two words, and the sidebar. No shot mixes them; the copy never sits over the product. Each card holds for about a second, long enough for a few words, and the words arrive whole and re-center rather than animating. The one exception is 'Tidy Tabs', which fades up, giving the name a slightly different entrance from the rest of the copy.

After the first three-second demo the cutting settles into an even pace: from 8.54 to 17.35 every shot lasts between 1.13 and 1.37 seconds. The music is not what sets it; cuts do not land on the estimated beat more often than chance. The film stops its product footage on the wavy line, cutting to the logo while the feature is still working, so the last thing in the sidebar is the button doing its job.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Reassure before you show the problem** (see 0:00). Open on the comforting line ('Don't worry') and then show the mess. The problem plays as something the viewer is allowed to do, not something they should fear.
- **Demo once in a single take** (see 0:07). Show the feature end to end without a cut the first time, including its working state, so the viewer trusts it is real.
- **Then repeat it with the wait cut out** (see 0:11). After the full demo, show it on other people's data with only the click or only the result. Repetition proves it generalizes; editing keeps it fast.
- **Name the feature after it has been seen** (see 0:09). Put the product name on screen right after the first demo, so it labels something the viewer already understands.
- **Fill the demo with this week's tabs** (see 0:11). Stock the demo with whatever people are actually reading right now. Placeholder content looks staged; topical content looks like the viewer's own browser.
- **Magnify the one control** (see 0:06). Crop to the part of the interface the feature touches and scale it up until a single row is headline size. Drop the rest of the window.

## Not the lesson here

There is no voice, no person on camera, no explanation of how the sorting works and no mention of AI. The music is a steady bed that the picture is not cut to. Study it for how to demonstrate a one-button feature fast: one honest take, then quick repeats.
