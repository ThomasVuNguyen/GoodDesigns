# Craft notes: How One of NYC’s Fastest-Growing Startups Scaled to $5 Billion

The Science of Scaling · 16:50 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wNnU2BJILPA

A 17-minute B2B explainer made as a day-in-the-life vlog. HubSpot's host, Payton Kirol, says up front that she has no idea what a go-to-market engineer is, then spends a day at Clay with Everett Berry, who runs its GTM engineering team: a 7 a.m. train out of New Jersey, a string of meetings, a customer walk in Bryant Park and a pub at the end. Along the way the film answers the question in steps: what the role is (a pickle analogy), why Clay invented it (a founder's history), what the team does all day, and how Clay splits it into two teams that feed each other.

What is worth studying is how it keeps a dry subject watchable for 17 minutes without a single formal sit-down interview. Three layers take turns: handheld vlog footage where the host is always in or next to the frame, a bare studio set where she explains things to camera, and a green chalkboard of clay-like icons and hand lettering that draws every idea as it is said. Running jokes (making Everett famous, losing Everett, a pickle she has never eaten) give the day a thread to follow between the explanations.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## A question the host doesn't know the answer to

The whole film is set up as one question asked by someone who admits she can't answer it: 'What do they actually do every day? I have no idea, which is exactly why I ended up here.' That puts the host on the viewer's side. When an engineer says 'I am the core user of every other company' and she says it is confusing, she says what the audience is thinking, and the film earns the right to explain.

The explanation is spread across the day instead of delivered in one block. The definition comes first, through an analogy (pickles and delis), then the history of why the role exists, then what the team does, and only in the last third the idea the film is really about: Clay has two GTM teams, and one feeds the other. Each step is triggered by something in the day, a person she meets or a line someone drops ('So there's two go-to-market engineering teams'), so the structure feels found rather than planned.

Running jokes carry the viewer between the explanations. 'Make Everett famous' starts on the train and pays off when coworkers photograph him. Losing Everett in meetings turns into a tour of the office. The pickle she has never eaten is set up at 3:50, promised in the sponsor read and paid off after the credits. Each gives viewers a small reason to keep watching that has nothing to do with GTM engineering.

- 0:25 `seconds/second-00026.webp` 'I have no idea, which is exactly why I ended up here.'
- 3:45 `seconds/second-00226.webp` The pickle jar: a setup that pays off fourteen minutes later.
- 12:24 `seconds/second-00745.webp` 'Wait, two teams?' The turn into the film's main idea.
- 16:40 `seconds/second-01001.webp` The pickle, after the credits.

## One host, three ways of speaking

Payton speaks in three registers, and the film switches between them constantly. In the bare studio she addresses the lens and restates what was just said in plain words. Over b-roll she narrates in the past tense like a diary ('The second we got to the office, Everett's dream of becoming famous started to come true'). On location she is a participant, asking questions and reacting. The studio is the one that does the teaching: after every expert line that would lose a newcomer, the film cuts to her saying it again more simply ('So what Spencer is describing is the core job of a go-to-market engineer').

The experts are never interviewed formally. Spencer sits beside her on a sofa, Varun stands at a whiteboard, Everett draws on another one, and George talks from his desk. Because the host is in or next to the frame, their answers play as conversation. Lower thirds name everyone with their exact title, and the important lines get extra help: quiet remarks get yellow italic subtitles, and jargon is spelled out in brackets ('build like a (Proof of Concept) for them').

Lines that would be dull as narration are given to the people who lived them. Varun tells how the role got its name in his own words, and the Slack message where he named it is shown as a card; Everett adds 'And then I didn't sell anything at Clay for six months.' The history becomes anecdotes told by its characters.

## Vlog camera, with a studio to come home to

Location footage is shot like a vlog: wide lenses close to faces, the camera walking with people, the crew acknowledged ('Are you under pressure because we're watching you?'). It is deliberately unpolished, and a fast-forward, a punch-in or a jump cut is used whenever a moment runs long. The commute plays almost entirely in one two-shot across the train seats, so the cutting comes from the conversation, not from coverage.

The studio is the opposite: a locked-off wide of an almost empty loft, the host in a chair with a corded mic, a filing cabinet, an old TV and some props on a rug. It is cut in two or three sizes so a long piece to camera never sits on one frame, and the props (pickles, the TV) feed the jokes. Going back to the same calm frame after every outing tells viewers they are about to get the explanation.

Clock stamps ('7:00am', '8:40am', '1:00pm', '2:30pm', '2:45pm') are the only device for time. They make a 17-minute edit feel like one continuous day, and at 9:37 three of them in four seconds compress an afternoon of meetings into a joke about how busy Everett is.

- 9:37 `seconds/second-00578.webp` Three clock stamps in four seconds.

## Two graphics packages and the client's own art

The graphics come in two clearly separate kits. The show's package is cream graph paper with tall condensed black type and green doodles; it is used only for things that belong to HubSpot, such as the title, the sponsor read and the end card. The explainer board is a dark green grid, like a chalkboard, where Clay's ideas get drawn: glossy clay-like icons (group figures, folders, tiles), white sans labels, and hand lettering for anything a person would write by hand ('WTF is a', 'SYSTEM', 'Personality'). Timelines and counters sit on white paper.

The clay look of the icons echoes the company's name and brand. Clay's real billboards, its logo wall and its clay-modelled landscape illustration all appear as found material, and the one-line product definition, 'Clay is the tool that lets GTMEs build those systems', is typed over the brand's own art. The explainer feels made for the client without the film having to say so.

Every graphic arrives in step with the voice. Lists type themselves one item per clause, labels snap on a word at a time, a dotted line runs from the GTME folder to its outputs as the narration gets there, and the camera slides across the board to follow a lead from one team to the other. The two teams keep their colors (blue-green internal, pink-orange forward deployed) from their first appearance to the final loop, so later diagrams need no labels to be read.

## The product, as the punchline to an analogy

Clay itself appears only a few times, and always right after something has been explained in plain terms. Spencer's pickle analogy ends on 'build a list of all of the delis'; the very next shot is a Clay prompt asking for 'a list of all the delis in nyc!! I wanna sell them pickles!!' and the table it returns. The analogy and the demo use the same words, so the screen needs no narration.

Elsewhere the product is shown as working material rather than as a polished UI: a table filling with rows for 'a spreadsheet that could pull data from anywhere', filter panels, a table with job titles boxed in red for the 'data test', and Everett's own whiteboard sketch of the founder-dinner workflow instead of a screen recording of it.

## A bed that stops for the jokes

Music runs under almost the whole film, roughly 5 to 15 dB below the voice by the stem analysis, and by the spectrogram it changes cue at many section breaks. It is not cut to: a tempo check found cuts landing on the beat no more often than chance. What the edit does with the music is take it away. The stem drops out for a second or more about a dozen times, nearly always on a punchline or a turn.

The longest gap is twelve seconds under Varun's 'How do we become an amazing company? … apparently that's what sales is' (7:19 to 7:31), so the founder's dry joke plays in silence. The music also stops for 'quick pause' before the sponsor read, for 'no one responded', and on 'he said something that stopped me in my tracks… Wait, two teams?' before the main explainer begins. Then it runs unbroken for almost four minutes under that explainer. The pickle tasting at the end has no music at all.

## Warmth that sells the explanation

The film keeps its awkward moments: Spencer's hype that falls flat ('This is not good hype'), 'That was slightly awkward', Everett vanishing into meetings, a customer meeting she is not allowed into. A sponsored film about a $5 billion company could easily feel like an ad. These moments make it feel like a real day, which makes the business claims around them easier to accept.

The confidential meeting shows this best. Instead of cutting it, the film shows it out of focus with 'CONFIDENTIAL' typed over it, and then cuts to the host leaning on a wall waiting. The limit on access becomes part of the story.

- 5:07 `seconds/second-00308.webp` 'This is not good hype.'
- 11:04 `seconds/second-00665.webp` The meeting shown blurred, stamped CONFIDENTIAL.
- 9:54 `seconds/second-00595.webp` Losing Everett turns into a tour.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Let the host not know** (see 0:25). Have the presenter admit up front that they can't answer the question, and let them say 'that's confusing' when an expert loses them. The audience follows someone learning, not someone lecturing.
- **Restate every expert line from a home base** (see 4:28). Keep one calm, locked-off set to return to. After each expert answer, cut there and have the host say it again in plain words. Viewers learn that this frame means 'here is the simple version'.
- **Make the analogy and the demo use the same words** (see 4:19). End an analogy on a concrete request and use that exact request as the product prompt in the next shot. The demo needs no voice-over.
- **Keep separate graphics kits for the show and the subject** (see 1:05). Give the show's own segments (title, sponsor, credits) one look and the subject's explainers another, so viewers always know whose voice a graphic speaks in.
- **Give recurring ideas fixed colors** (see 12:41). Assign each team or concept a color on first appearance and never change it, so later diagrams can drop their labels.
- **Take the music out for the punchline** (see 7:21). Run a quiet bed under everything and cut it for a second or more on the jokes and turns. Silence does the emphasis that a sting would overdo.
- **Turn a limit on access into a scene** (see 11:04). When you can't film something, show that you can't: blur it, label it, and cut to the host waiting outside.
- **Use clock stamps to hold a long edit together** (see 9:37). Time stamps over establishing shots make a long, cut-down day feel continuous, and a burst of them makes a quick joke about how full the day was.

## Not the lesson here

There are no formal sit-down interviews, no cinematic coverage of the office and no music worth studying on its own; the cuts are not timed to the beat. Study it for keeping a jargon-heavy subject watchable at length, by cutting between vlog, studio and drawn explanation.
