# Craft notes: 5 times scientists experimented on themselves - Shannon Odell

TED-Ed · 6:16 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fqL9dt6QHWg

A TED-Ed lesson on five Nobel laureates who tested their discoveries on themselves: George de Hevesy, Werner Forssmann, Max Theiler, Tu Youyou and Barry Marshall, with his partner Robin Warren. Shannon Odell wrote it, Caitlin McCarthy directed and animated it, and Addison Anderson narrates over a score by cAMP Studio. It runs 6:16, with five and a half minutes of story.

It is worth studying for two things. First, how a list of five unrelated stories becomes one film: a cold open that tells the whole first story before the premise, a book stack that lays out what is coming, short and long stories in different shapes, and one image to close them all. Second, how flat 2D animation explains procedures nobody would want to film. Every step of a catheter, a vaccine or a stomach infection is drawn as a single clear action, organs get faces, repetition multiplies the frame, and almost every scene change grows out of a shape that is already on screen.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## Five stories, three shapes

The lesson never opens with its premise. It opens on de Hevesy in bed with indigestion, and tells his whole story in under a minute: suspicion, accusation, a hidden test, proof, and the Nobel Prize thirty years later. Only at 1:09, after the anecdote has paid off, does the narrator say what the film is about. The viewer has already seen one example of the pattern, so the premise lands as a generalization rather than a promise.

Then the film shows its plan. The premise sequence ends on a glowing stack of books whose spines read FORSSMANN, THEILER, Youyou and WARREN & MARSHALL, a table of contents the viewer can read in the two seconds it holds. It is the only place the four remaining names appear together, and it means nobody has to wonder how long the lesson will run.

The remaining stories do not get equal time. Forssmann gets about 90 seconds and a full arc: idea, objection, the act, repetition, disgrace, war, and vindication by other people. Theiler and Youyou share one introduction ('Two other laureates also put their bodies on the line') and get about half a minute each, with no setback. Marshall gets nearly 90 seconds and is set up as the twist: the only one who experimented 'not to show something was safe, but to prove that it was dangerous'. Long, short, short, long keeps a list of five from feeling like a list.

Each story still runs on the same beats: a problem, someone refusing to help (the landlady, colleagues, failed animal trials), the self-dose, the proof, and the prize. The repetition is what lets the short stories be short. By Theiler the viewer knows the shape and only needs the details.

## A plain script with a few jokes

One narrator, Addison Anderson, carries every word. Nobody in the film speaks, so the characters act entirely in pantomime: the landlady's eruption, the colleagues' raised hands, Youyou's thumbs up. The narration states facts and the picture adds attitude, so the film can be funny without the script ever winking.

The few jokes in the script are timed for the picture. 'Marshall took matters into his own... stomach' leaves a gap where 'hands' should go, and the film fills it with Marshall raising a finger and the score dropping out, then a pat on the belly. 'Marshall's decision to trust his gut' comes as his tie closes and the crowd of saved lives rises, a pun the picture leaves alone. And 'not something just anyone should try at home' closes a film that has spent six minutes making self-experimentation look heroic.

Numbers are kept to a minimum. There are only a few years (1929, the 1930s, 1945, 1979, the 1940s) and two short counts ('eight more times', 'within five days' and 'after 10 days'). The one year the film puts on screen is 1943, rolled up from 1913 like an odometer, because the thirty-year wait for de Hevesy's prize is the point.

## Grainy color, glowing outlines, organs with faces

The look is a short list of saturated colors: ultramarine and navy for night, interiors and the inside of the body, lemon yellow, coral red, bubblegum pink, mint and one bottle green. Every gradient is grainy, and outlines carry a soft colored halo with a slight color fringe, as if the drawing were glowing on an old screen. It reads as warm and handmade even in the darkest scenes.

Color does a job. Lemon yellow with a glow marks whatever is active or dangerous: the radioactive tracer, the Geiger counter's crackle, the yellow fever virus and its mosquito, Theiler's vaccine, the cloud of H. pylori and Marshall's drink. Each laureate has fixed colors (Forssmann's yellow skin and green trousers, Theiler's pale blue skin and purple suit, Youyou's red skin and yellow jacket, Marshall's blue shirt and red tie), so they can be recognized from far away, inside a coin or in the final group shot. Forssmann's disgrace is shown in color against line: he stays in full color while his colleagues become pale blue outlines around him.

The body is drawn as characters. The heart has a face and changes expression as the catheter travels, kidneys look alarmed when the tracer arrives, the parasite is a blue ball with yellow arms, a stressed stomach grows a worried face, and Marshall's healthy gut smiles with sparkles. Procedures that would be hard to watch become readable and even funny, without losing what each step does.

There are no photographs, archival portraits or real places anywhere. The only words on screen are part of drawn objects: 'Lidocaine' on a tube, 'X-RAY DEPT.' on a sign, the names on book spines, 医 and 'Sweet wormwood' on book covers, and 'helicobacter pylori' spelled out by the bacteria themselves.

## Scene changes that grow out of shapes

The film almost never just cuts from one idea to the next. A studio backdrop is pulled away to reveal a giant heart (1:45). Forssmann's disgrace closes into a heart-shaped iris that cracks and falls apart onto his wartime ward (2:33 to 2:36). The demonstration of the catheter shrinks into a coin that flips to Theiler, then to Youyou, then to a hazard sign (3:09 to 3:13). The hazard sign's silhouette fills in as Theiler, and its starburst becomes the virus in his hands. The sweet wormwood's stem becomes the line diagram of its molecule, and the molecule's circle becomes the parasite.

The camera travels through bodies and objects to change scale. It drops through the floor from bedroom to kitchen, tilts down the kidneys' tubes into a river, pulls back from an infected cell to the flushed face of a fever patient, pulls back from a parasite to a hole in a mouse, and dives into Marshall's stomach by lifting his tie, a move the film repeats at 5:37 to close his story. Each move tells the viewer that the next image is a closer or wider view of the same thing.

Repetition is shown by multiplying the frame. One plate that crackles becomes a table of crackling plates, one forearm becomes a wall of forearms for 'eight more times', and one mouth with a pill becomes a row of mouths when the antibiotic treatment becomes standard. Time passing is a window flipping between sun and moon, at the boarding house for 'days later' and over Marshall's bed for ten days, with the camera locked off so only the window and the patient change.

Camera moves advance in steps about an eighth of a second apart, so FFmpeg's cut detector finds 222 cuts, many of which are the steps of one pan or push. The real cutting is slower and often sits on a held composition: Cournand and Richards glance at each other for three seconds, Youyou holds still with her eyes closed after the dose, the final tower holds for fifteen.

## A steady score that steps back for the big beats

cAMP Studio's score (composer Salil Bhayani, sound designer Anthony Nguyen) plays under almost every second of narration, mostly 7 to 15 dB under the voice. It is a bed, not a commentary, and the music's estimated tempo does not line up with the cuts any better than chance.

What matters is where it gets out of the way. The score thins as de Hevesy's story ends (about 1:04 to 1:11) and comes back as the premise begins. It drops out almost completely for about four seconds around 1:42, under Forssmann's 'radical idea', and returns as the backdrop is pulled away to show the heart. It sinks again from about 4:08 to 4:17, under Youyou's decision and dose, and comes back as the narration moves on to Marshall. And it disappears for two seconds at 5:00, so 'his own... stomach' lands in near silence.

The film ends the same way. The score stops under the last line, there is a second and a half of near silence around 5:55 over the held tower, and a short closing sting carries the credits. Demucs puts most of that sting in its voice stem, but recognition finds no words in it.

## Something to watch every second

A six-minute lesson about five people could easily sag in the middle, and this one keeps it moving in three ways. Each story has a small physical gag that has nothing to do with the facts: the landlady's starburst, the mosquito as big as Theiler before a wide shot shows him swatting it, the sulking pig with a stethoscope, the pill multiplying into a chorus of mouths. Each one is short, and none of them needs the narration.

Images also make the next line easy to predict. Lemon yellow keeps appearing, so the viewer learns to look for it. The window with the sun and moon returns when time passes, the medal returns when a prize is mentioned, and books return for study and knowledge. By the end, a stack of books and a gold medal tell the viewer what is coming before the narrator does.

And the film pays its own setups off. The tie that opened into Marshall's stomach closes again at the end of his story. The book stack from the premise becomes the base of the final tower. The six laureates, met one at a time, appear together only once, holding their medals in the last group shot, so the ending feels like the closing of the table of contents the film showed at 1:29.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Tell one whole story before the premise** (see 0:06). Open on the most concrete, smallest example and finish it, prize and all, before saying what the film is about. The premise then lands as the lesson of something the viewer has just seen.
- **Put the table of contents in the world** (see 1:29). Show what is coming as an object the characters live in, like names on book spines, held long enough to read. The viewer knows the length and order without a title card.
- **Vary the story lengths** (see 3:10). In a list of stories, give the strongest two full arcs and compress the others into a shared introduction. Long, short, short, long keeps a list from feeling like a list.
- **Grow the next scene out of a shape** (see 3:13). Change scenes through something already on screen: a backdrop pulled away, an iris that breaks, a coin that flips, a silhouette that fills in. Each change also says what the next scene has to do with this one.
- **Multiply the frame for repetition** (see 2:19). When the narration says something happened many times, or to many people, duplicate the image across the frame instead of showing it again in sequence.
- **Give organs faces** (see 1:50). Draw the body's parts as characters that react to the procedure. Each step becomes an expression, and a hard procedure becomes something people will watch.
- **Step back the music for the beat that matters** (see 5:00). Keep the score steady under the voice, and pull it out for the decision, the dose or the joke. The silence marks the moment better than a sting would.
- **Close the gag you opened** (see 5:37). Reuse a striking move at the end of a story, like the tie that opens into the stomach and later closes over it, so the story ends where it began.

## Not the lesson here

There are no photographs, archival portraits, real places, maps, charts or on-screen captions naming people and dates, and nobody speaks except the narrator. Do not study it for collage or documentary evidence; study it for structuring several short biographies into one film, drawing medical procedures clearly, and changing scenes through shapes.
