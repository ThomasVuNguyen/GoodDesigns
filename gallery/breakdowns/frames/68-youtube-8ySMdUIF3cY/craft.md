# Craft notes: Starship - Go For Orbit

SpaceX · 21:10 · https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8ySMdUIF3cY

A 21-minute company film about one flight: Starship Flight 14, captioned 'September 28, 2026 | First orbital mission'. It opens on the moment things go wrong. During the ascent an engine shuts down, the loop says 'we are not committing to orbit', and only then does the title come up. The rest of the film rewinds to dawn, catches up with that call at five and a half minutes, and then follows how, by its own account, the team reversed it, chose where to land and brought the ship back.

What is worth studying is how it builds tension and scale with almost nothing invented. There is no narrator and no score worth naming. Engineers explain the stakes in interviews, the mission loop reports what happens in clipped present-tense calls, the rooms in Starbase and Redmond react, and when people speak too quietly to hear, the film subtitles them. The vehicle is almost never shown without something to measure it by: a horizon, a tower, a person, the Earth.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## Open on the setback, then earn it back

The film starts at its lowest point. Over the first 48 seconds of engine parts being made, one engineer says the Raptor is impressive, complex and 'pretty scary' to rely on. Then the flight: staging, boostback, a call acknowledging Engine 6, an 'ORCU controller reset', and 'we are not committing to orbit'. Only after that does the title appear. When the film rewinds to dawn, the viewer already knows the problem is coming, so the question is never what happens but what these people will do about it. At 5:24 the loop repeats the cold open's call over the ship in space, and the story has caught up with itself.

From there it is built around three decisions, and each one follows the same shape: an engineer explains the stakes in an interview, the room argues, and the loop delivers the outcome. Jeremy Pedro explains the conservative flight rule and Shana Diez the risk calculus of a development program before 'We have exec override and concurrence'. The landing-site interview and a subtitled room debate come before 'Confirming we're going to commit to Hawaii'. Zac Strimbu on deorbit testing comes before 'Deorbit burn startup'. The viewer is always taught what could go wrong just before it might.

The apparent detours are placed to pay off. The reflown heat tile sets up survival and reuse. The Starlink factory chapter explains why the payload mattered to the people in Redmond. The longest detour, the history of Raptor testing, sits between 'commit to Hawaii' and the deorbit burn, so it both explains why the burn is frightening (the engine has to relight after a long coast) and makes the viewer wait for it. The YouTube chapters follow these turns closely.

The emotional peak is not the launch or the splashdown. It is 'Deorbit burn shutdown', when a room of controllers who have been shown in tight close-ups for over a minute finally lets go. The splashdown gets eight seconds near the end with no cheering, and the last shot is a ship on the tower under a rehearsal poll for a return to launch site. That bookends the very first line, about 'a historic return to launch site mission', and leaves the film on the next question rather than a victory lap.

## Interviews explain, the loop reports, the room reacts

There is no narrator. The script is assembled from four sources, and each has one job. Eleven named people, captioned with their titles, explain in past tense and plain words ('So everything looked great until Engine 6 went out'). The mission loop reports in clipped present tense, full of jargon nobody stops to define: E6, ORCU, Burn 2, 'exec override and concurrence'. The rooms in Starbase and Redmond react. On-screen text does the rest: locator titles with the time of day, one stat line for orbit, and three black chapter cards, 'PAYLOAD', 'TRAJECTORY' and 'DEORBIT BURN'.

The division matters because the interviews never give away an outcome. Everyone on camera knows how the flight ended, but the result always arrives through the loop, as a live call. That is how a film made after the fact keeps its suspense: the people who know explain the stakes, and the record delivers the news.

When the room talks too quietly to hear, the film subtitles it. 'There's a pneumatics leak.' 'This is why we need more engines on the ship.' During the landing debate: 'What are you most worried about?' 'Engine shutdown is an elevated risk.' 'Hawaii has a really good runway.' 'E also wants to go to Hawaii.' The subtitles are sparse, used only where the speech is otherwise lost, and they turn murmured asides into evidence that the decision was argued by people, not announced.

The customer gets a voice too. The Redmond satellite team hears 'not committing' and says it plainly: 'That would be the end of the show.' Later, Richard Bielski thanks the Starship team for giving them 'a little heart attack'. Names also carry across the film: 'Jake was the final go for the engine systems' at 7:26, and Jake Berkowitz turns up ten minutes later in a hard hat explaining frost on a thrust chamber.

## Always give the vehicle a ruler

The film almost never shows the rocket on its own. It measures it against something you already know the size of. At dawn the ship's nose rises through a flat horizon. It stands between dark towers, beside a floodlit wall, reflected in standing water under a big sky. From the control room it is seen through the window over a row of heads, and a controller raises binoculars to look at it. In orbit it is the Earth's curve, and the satellites drifting off toward it. At the end it is a speck under cloud, filmed from the sea.

The best single example is a twenty-second unbroken shot at 3:48. It starts on one bare heat tile in a field of black hexagons, the tile the film has just watched being tested, handled and pressed on by a technician. The camera pulls back and sinks until the tile is a speck on the ship's skirt, then keeps going down the booster to the tower and the sea. A thing you could hold becomes the unit you measure the rocket by.

People are framed the opposite way. In mission control the camera is tight: faces half lit by screens, hands at mouths, brows and headsets, often one person to a frame. During the deorbit burn they come in a long run, one after another. The alternation does the work, a vehicle too big for the frame, then a face that fills it. It also keeps the drama on the people, which is where the story puts it.

Light and time of day add weight. The vehicle is mostly shown at dawn, dusk or night, as a silhouette or under floodlights. The pad fades up from black at 4:14 as a column of lights, and locators turn the day into a clock: 5:57 AM in Redmond, 7:22 AM on launch day.

## Where the score steps out

The music is not the point. An uncredited score runs under most of the film, usually sitting 7 to 14 dB below the interviews, and the cuts land on its beat no more often than chance would give. What is worth learning is where it stops. At 4:13.6 the score cuts with a fade to black, the pad fades up as a column of lights, and for twelve seconds there is only room tone and loop calls. The score comes back at 4:25.5, on the cut to the control room.

The orbital burn is handled the same way. By the stems, a pulsing cue under the go/no-go poll stops at 8:03.8, right on 'Burn 2 startup'. What follows is low rumble under the engine shots, then near silence from about 8:22 to 8:30 under 'No alerts or alarms on the board'. Music creeps back at about 8:30, the 'ORBITAL' lower third lands at 8:32 and 'We're in orbit' at 8:34.6. Elsewhere the track gives way to the real thing: about 22 seconds of launch roar from 4:54, and natural sound at the McGregor test site until an engine fires at 16:32.

## Leave the problems in

This is a company film about its own flight, so everything in it is the company's account. What makes it credible is how much it leaves in. It opens on the failure. It keeps the jargon unexplained and the loop calls clipped. It keeps the swearing: 'It's like 90% of the shit that we want', 'are we fucking up and missing something?', 'Fuck yeah' when the burn ends. It keeps onboard video tearing into streaks at 1:08 and breaking into blocks at 15:09. It shows the CEO questioning the requirements and an older controller doing the talking while he rubs his forehead.

It shows the plan B. The original call not to proceed is explained, not hidden; the customer's doubt is on camera; the landing site is chosen by argument, with the risks named out loud. Numbers are specific and sparing: 26 satellites, 17 minutes of coast, 30 minutes to decide, a nine-hour mission to Chile, '26,373 km/hr | Altitude 276 km'. Every person who explains something is named with a real job title, and the closing montage of earlier flights includes vehicles exploding. The failures are in the highlight reel.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Open on the crisis, then rewind** (see 1:50). Start at the worst moment, put the title after it, then go back to the beginning. The viewer stops asking what happens and starts asking how these people will handle it.
- **Let interviews pose the question and the record answer it** (see 7:02). Have the people who know the outcome explain only the stakes, in past tense. Deliver every result through live audio, so a film made afterwards still plays as if it is happening now.
- **Subtitle what the room says under its breath** (see 13:24). When real talk is too quiet to hear, caption it, sparingly. Overheard asides read as evidence and show a decision being argued, not announced.
- **Give every big object a ruler** (see 3:56). Frame the vehicle against a horizon, a person, a window or the Earth, and once, pull back in a single take from something you could hold to the whole machine.
- **Delay the payoff with the backstory it depends on** (see 15:13). Put the explanation of why the next moment is dangerous right before it, so the detour both teaches and makes the viewer wait.
- **Cut the music where the outcome is decided** (see 8:25). Let the score stop on the call that starts the critical moment and leave the loop and room tone alone until the result lands.
- **Put the peak on the decision, not the spectacle** (see 19:43). Spend the big emotional release on the moment that mattered to the people, and give the spectacle that follows a few quiet seconds.
- **End on the next question** (see 21:03). Close on a quiet image and a line that points at the next mission, ideally one that echoes the first line of the film.

## Not the lesson here

There is no narrator, no animation beyond one globe and a lower-third system, and no music worth studying for its own sake. It is not a film for motion graphics, product screens or score; it is a film for structure, sourcing a script from real audio, and making size felt.
