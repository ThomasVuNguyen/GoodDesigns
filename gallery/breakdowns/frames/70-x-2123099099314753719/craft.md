# Craft notes: Introducing Helios: measuring 1,000+ protein biomarkers from a drop of blood to help you…

Omar Abudayyeh (@omarabudayyeh) · 1:19 · https://x.com/omarabudayyeh/status/2123099099314753719

A seventy-nine-second launch film that works like a good pitch. It asks one question ('why is our health still a guessing game?'), lets two founders answer it in turn from their lab, shows the product in a stranger's hands at home, and then, just when a viewer's doubt would peak, says the doubt out loud: 'And yes, we know tech has made big promises about a single drop of blood before.'

What makes it worth studying is how many kinds of picture it holds together. Borrowed clips on an old TV, a lab interview with two cameras, staged scenes in warm homes and press clippings all sit under the same burned-in subtitles and the same green glass graphics. The script decides which kind of picture each line gets, and the graphics make them read as one film.

Frames are in `seconds/second-NNNNN.webp`, where NNNNN is the whole second plus one.

## Question, promise, proof, then the doubt

The film is a pitch in eleven short sections, most under ten seconds. A question, the problem in the viewer's own terms ('You do everything you're supposed to'), the promise, where it comes from, the product, how it works in three steps (collect, measure, guide), the objection, the sign-off. Each section has one job, and each changes the kind of picture on screen, so the structure can be felt even with the sound off.

The order of the proof is the smart part. The objection comes only after the product has been shown in full, when a viewer has seen enough to have the doubt, and it is answered before the ending, so the last thing heard is not the doubt but 'Your body is telling a story. We've made a new way to listen.' The film also opens on a question and closes on an invitation to 'Find out', which makes the whole thing feel like the answer.

## Two founders, one script, and the doubt said out loud

Two co-founders share the script, captioned once by name when they first appear: Omar Abudayyeh and Jonathan Gootenberg. Matching the voice stem against the shots where each is plainly talking shows the split. Omar asks the opening question before he is seen, Jonathan names the problem, Omar makes the promise and walks through how it works, Jonathan introduces the product and the AI agent, Omar takes the objection, and Jonathan signs off. Nobody is being interviewed. They deliver written lines straight to the lens, standing together, and the edit carries a line off their faces onto pictures and often back again.

The writing is plain and spoken. Short sentences, second person for the problem ('disease can still creep up on you long before you feel it'), 'we' for the company. The lists come in threes ('how your body is aging, how your organs are functioning, and your risk of developing disease'), and each item gets its own highlight on screen. The key phrase is placed with care. Jonathan says 'from a single drop of blood' straight into the lens with one finger raised, after his line has left his face for the unboxing and come back, and the music cuts out under it.

Then the film does what most launch films avoid. Twenty-five seconds later Omar, in the plainest frame with both founders in shot, says 'And yes, we know tech has made big promises about a single drop of blood before.' It names nobody and does not need to. Repeating the headline phrase word for word turns it from a promise into the viewer's own objection, said first by the people with most to lose. A two-word question, 'The difference?', sets up the answer, and the answer is split: the voice says 'This technology has been validated by labs and companies around the world', while the screen shows credentials and press clippings.

On-screen text is the third voice. Subtitles are burned in throughout, so the film reads with the sound off as feeds autoplay. 'Do it yourself, Needle free, Painless' is never spoken; it fills the one pause the voices take after the opening. And the subtitles are promoted to titles twice, at the two ends of the argument: 'a guessing game?' in the middle of black, and 'Your body is telling a story.' and 'Find out with Helios.' set large beside Jonathan.

## Each kind of picture has a job

The film uses three kinds of picture and gives each one a job. Borrowed clips show the world as it is: technology flickering on an old TV, then three wordless seconds of hospital (an x-ray, records, a held hand, walker wheels), graded dark and cold. The lab is for the people: the founders and the bench work behind them, in cool window light. Staged scenes at home are for the product: daylight, wood, plants, oranges on a board. Cutting from one kind to another tells the viewer that the subject has changed.

The interview coverage is simple and disciplined. The front two-shot is home base, and a low three-quarter angle covers the hand-off between speakers. Solo close-ups are saved for three lines: Jonathan's 'Now we've turned that work into Helios', his 'single drop of blood', and Omar's 'and companies around the world'. Each founder is alone in frame only when he makes a claim he has to own.

Lines often loop from face to pictures and back. Jonathan's product line starts in close-up, plays over the unboxing and the device, and returns to his face for its last words. Omar's measuring section ends back on the two-shot for 'decades into the future', and the press-clipping run ends on his close-up. The faces punctuate the graphics, so the film never goes long without a person in it.

## One green glass layer over every kind of footage

The graphics are a small, strict kit. A deep green blurred field (behind the x-ray panel, the clippings and the end card); frosted glass cards with rounded corners and a thin white edge; tiny spaced capitals with a dot for labels ('• Heart', '• Your plan', '• Helios AI', 'Muscle strength score'); big light numerals; and headlines that set the plain words light and the claim bold, as in 'Built by physicians and scientists'. Every word, from the founders' names to the bullets, the cards and the closing lines, fades on the same way, one word at a time.

How the kit meets live action is the lesson. Cards sit in the empty part of a frame (the lower right of the kitchen, the window behind the yoga pose), never over a face. One card survives a cut, so a yoga shot and a dumbbell shot read as one measured workout. The x-ray panel begins as a real woman's silhouette that dissolves into a translucent body, so the data looks like hers. The numbers agree everywhere: a whole-body age of 26 against a real age of 31 on the panel, the phone and the strength card, as if one person's results ran through the whole film.

The ending turns the logo into the last image in a visual rhyme. A red drop strikes a surface, water ripples out in rings, a tree's cross-section shows its years, a fingertip shows its whorls, and a white dot on that fingertip bursts into the logo's concentric rings. Each is a pattern that records something, which is what the line before it promised: 'Your body is telling a story.' The brand mark arrives as the answer, not as a sign-off slapped on the end.

## The music steps out for one line

Most of the time the music is a bed. One cue with a steady pulse (about 178 BPM by estimate, which may be felt at half that) sits about 6 to 17 dB under the voices, and the cutting does not follow its beat: 11 of 34 cuts land near a beat, fewer than chance would give. The opening is denser, with bursts of broadband noise that, by the spectrogram, sit under the TV's channel changes, and it thins at about six seconds when the film cuts to the hospital.

One move is clearly designed. In the second-long pause after 'where your health is headed', while the woman presses the device to her arm, the music swells by about 17 dB. On the cut to Jonathan's raised finger at 33.2 it stops dead, and 'from a single drop of blood' is heard almost alone. The cue comes back on the cut to the bullets at 34.4. The claim the film will later defend is the one moment it is given silence. At the end the music fades from about 73.5 and is gone by 75.7, leaving the end card silent for three seconds.

## Credibility earned, and credibility borrowed

Much of the trust is earned on screen. The founders are named, stand in a working lab with a scientist at the bench behind them, and say the lines themselves. The specifics are concrete, as the film states them: 'less than five minutes', 'over a thousand biomarkers', a plan that reads like a real one (creatine, vitamin D, lights out by 11 pm). The device is shown in macro with blood in its channel, not kept out of frame. And the biggest risk to the pitch, the memory of an earlier blood-testing promise, is raised by the founders before a viewer can raise it.

Some of it is borrowed, and it is worth seeing how. While the voice says 'This technology has been validated by labs and companies around the world', the screen shows clippings about the founders' earlier CRISPR work: detecting HPV and Zika, a gene technology from MIT, at-home coronavirus tests. They vouch for the team more than for the protein measurements Helios sells, and the cut lets one stand in for the other. It works at speed, about a second per clipping. Anyone borrowing the move should know that a careful viewer may notice the gap.

## Techniques to reuse

- **Say the objection before the viewer does** (see 0:58). After the product is fully shown, have the founders name the obvious doubt in the words of your own headline, then answer it at once. Put it in your plainest frame, with everyone in shot.
- **Split the script by role between two speakers** (see 0:13). Give one founder the problem and the product, the other the promise, the method and the defence. Hand off on a cut to a second angle so the turn feels natural.
- **Pull the music for the hardest claim** (see 0:33). Swell the cue in the pause before your boldest line, then cut it dead on a close-up so the claim is heard almost alone.
- **Let the highlight follow the voice** (see 0:44). Lay a still diagram out in full and brighten only the labels the voice is naming, in the order it names them.
- **Keep an overlay across a cut** (see 0:54). Leave a graphic card in place when the shot changes; two shots of the same person then read as one session and one measurement.
- **Let text carry what the voice skips** (see 0:35). Use the voice's pauses for short on-screen lists of facts nobody needs to hear spoken.
- **Promote the subtitle to a title at the ends** (see 1:07). Burn in subtitles throughout, then lift the opening question and the closing line out of the caption band and set them large in the frame.
- **Make the logo the last image in a rhyme** (see 1:14). Find real images that share the shape of your mark and cut them in a short chain, so the logo arrives as the answer.

## Not the lesson here

No customers or patients speak, there is no explanation of how one drop yields a thousand measurements, and no study figures, accuracy numbers or prices appear. Do not study it for explaining science; study it for scripting, interview coverage and how one graphics layer ties mixed footage together.
