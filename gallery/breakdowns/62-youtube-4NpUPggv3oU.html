Ideas are everywhere

Lovable · 01:00 film

Watch original ↗

Lovable's one-minute brand film, told by one narrator. It opens on small glowing windows of footage and rim-lit silhouettes with the reasons ideas die written across their faces, then types the Lovable name into its own prompt box and builds app after app in a single window, each change carried by a smear of the brand gradient. The breakdown follows how the voice, the on-screen words and the typed prompts share the script, and samples 14 UI moves as flip books.

Craft

A one-minute film that spends its first fifteen seconds on people, not product. Ideas are everywhere, it says over small windows of homes and streets; then it shows why they die, eleven reasons written across the faces of silhouettes with no faces. 'Until today' is the hinge, and the rest is proof: the Lovable name typed into its own prompt box, an app built from one sentence, and a run of apps, sign-ups, payments and edits made in front of you.

What is worth studying is how the film turns a claim nobody can see, that the software it makes is real, into things you watch happen. The same user who signs up shows up in the database. The prompts are written the way people talk. And the motion is built from the product's own parts, so even the transitions look like Lovable.

The map lays the film's layers against its running time. Every segment plays the film from that point. The same notes are in craft.md for reading as text.

Story and structure

A problem with faces, then a pile of proof

The first quarter of the film is about people: where ideas happen, and why they die. It is dim, mostly dark and quiet, and it never mentions software. 'Until today' is the hinge, and the name arrives at fifteen seconds.

The remaining forty-five seconds are ordered like answers to a sceptic. Can it make anything? A resume, a meeting tool, a dashboard, a calendar, a marketplace. Is it real, or a demo? A booking is clicked through, an account is created and appears in the database. Can I change it? By asking, or by hand. Can it grow? Payments, AI, Publish. Then the feature list, a collage of everything, and the end line.

The last line, 'For every idea, there's Lovable', answers the first one, 'Ideas are everywhere'. The film closes on the word it opened with.

  1. 'Feels impossible' as the light fails: the problem ends in darkness.

  2. 'Until today' in the brand gradient, the hinge of the film.

  3. 'This isn't just a mock-up or a demo' starts over a site being clicked through.

  4. The end line comes back to 'idea', the film's first word.

Voices and script

One narrator, two scripts on screen

The narrator speaks in short sentences and fragments: 'In homes, on commutes, at 2 a.m.' 'Until today.' 'Built in minutes.' On screen, words do two different jobs. Some echo the voice exactly ('Ideas are everywhere', 'In homes', 'Until today'), blurring in as each word is said, so the opening reads like captions with weight.

Others say what the narrator does not. While the voice gives one general reason, 'Building was just too hard', the screen lists eleven specific ones across the silhouettes: 'No coding skills', 'Kids start school', 'Parents say no', 'Twelve hour shifts', 'Small town', 'Burnout'. This is where the film's warmth lives. These are ordinary, domestic reasons, and the viewer finds their own among them without the narrator having to list them.

The third voice is the user's. The prompts are typed in plain language, the way someone would ask a colleague: 'Create an AI resume builder', 'Make it easy and fun for an 8-year-old to use', 'Please add a premium subscription to my app for $12/month'. The narrator says 'Just tell Lovable' over one being sent. The voice then steps away twice: for two seconds of music over the chatbot and Publish, and for the end line, which is only written.

  1. 'No coding skills' written where the face should be, while the voice says something else.

  2. 'Parents say no': one of eleven reasons the narrator never reads out.

  3. 'Make it easy and fun for an 8-year-old to use': the request in ordinary words.

  4. Even the payment request says 'Please'.

The world and its graphics

A black stage, a gradient horizon, the interface as type

Almost every frame sits on black with Lovable's gradient glowing along the bottom edge, blue into pink into orange, like light on a horizon. The opening holds that color back. The footage plays in small glowing windows in the dark, each cut to the shape of its clip, and the silhouettes are lit only at their edges. The gradient first appears inside two words, when it sweeps through 'Until today', and from then on it is the light source of the film: the send button fills with it, the loading flower is made of it, and app changes are wiped by it.

Interface parts are set into the type as if they were words. 'Real' sits beside a toggle that switches on; 'product' follows a stack of avatars; a globe sits between 'Custom' and 'Domains'; the GitHub mark sits inside 'Code Ownership'; and the end line finishes on a heart that becomes the logo. The product's visual language becomes the film's alphabet.

Some screens are placed in rendered rooms: a laptop on a terrazzo desk with a vase and books, the booking calendar floating over the same desk, a laptop on a stone plinth under blue light. These moments take the interface out of the abstract black and put it on a desk where someone could use it.

  1. A small glowing window of lit apartments, captioned at its center.

  2. The brand's gradient first appears inside the words.

  3. A toggle, just switched on, set beside 'Real' as if it were a word.

  4. The booking calendar floats over a terrazzo desk.

  5. The globe icon set between two words.

Showing the product

One window, edited while you watch

The product is shown as a chain, not a gallery. A window holds still with a prompt pill docked at its bottom left. The next request starts typing while the current app is still on screen; the cursor presses send; a smear of gradient passes over; the new app is there. A meeting summarizer, a revenue dashboard, a family calendar and a creator marketplace follow each other in about five seconds, and because the frame never moves, it reads as one tool doing many jobs.

The claim 'real working product' is proved through continuity. An account is created with the name Laura Rose, and one shot later the 'Create account' button turns into a database row for 'laura.r@mail.me' that tilts into the table of users. A tour is booked by clicking 'Book Tour' and picking a date. A payment is made, 'Pay $12.00', and a 'Success' pill slides in. Every action has a cursor and a pressed state, so the screens read as being used rather than presented.

People come back inside the product. The opening's silhouettes had no faces and no names; the apps are full of them: a resume for 'Megan Reed', creators with portraits, an agent on the booking card, avatars beside 'product', and two named cursors, 'Jonathan' and 'Jodie', under 'Collaboration'. The film's problem is anonymous, and its product is populated.

  1. The next request typing under the current app: request and result overlap.

  2. 'Create your account' with Laura Rose in the field.

  3. The same account, now a row in the database.

  4. A real font menu, with the headline already changed.

  5. 'Success' after a $12 payment: the feature works on screen.

Music and sound

Low under the problem, lifted for the product

There is no credited music, and by the stems the soundtrack is one voice over one music cue, with no sound effects that stand out from it. The first line is spoken over near silence and the music enters at about 1.5 seconds. Under the problem it is kept well back, about 10 to 12 dB under the voice. At about 20 seconds, as the first app opens, the low end thickens and the music rises, sitting only 4 to 5 dB under the voice for the rest of the demo. The film's mood turns at the same moment its picture does.

Three later moves are placed with care. The music plays alone for about two seconds (50.5 to 52.8) over the chatbot and Publish. It thins to a break under 'Everything you need, all in one place', so the summary line and the six feature names stand clear, and comes back for the collage. Then it fades out from about 58.4, so the logo arrives at 58.75 in near silence. The tempo is near 120 BPM by estimate (it may feel half or double that), but the cutting does not follow it: 7 of 27 cuts land near a beat, about what chance would give.

  1. The music thickens as the first app opens.

  2. The break under the feature list.

  3. The wordmark lands as the music fades out.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Write the reasons, say the summary

    Let the narrator say one general line and put the specific, personal examples on screen at reading speed. Viewers find their own reason without the voice getting longer.

  2. Hold color back until the turn

    Keep the problem dim and dark, then let the brand color enter inside the words of the turn. The color change marks the hinge before the logo does.

  3. Type the brand name into the product

    Introduce the name as text typed into the product's own input, then delete it and type the first real request. The logo and the demo become one move.

  4. Keep the window, change the app

    Hold one frame still and change only what is inside it, with the next request typing before the current result has finished. Many outputs read as one tool.

  5. Carry one record across shots

    To prove something works, follow a single piece of data through it: the name typed at sign-up should be the row that lands in the database.

  6. Set interface parts inside the type

    Use a toggle, an avatar stack or an icon as a word in a headline. It keeps feature slides in the product's own language.

  7. Leave the last line unspoken

    Let the voice finish on the summary and give the closing line to the screen alone as the music fades. The quiet makes the logo the last thing heard and seen.

Not the lesson here. There are no real customers, testimonials, usage numbers or on-camera interviews; after the opening every face is demo data inside an app. It is not a film to study for documentary footage or for cutting to the beat.

Motion

Almost nothing here is a plain cut. The world is a black stage with Lovable's blue, pink and orange gradient glowing along the bottom of the frame like a horizon, and the moving parts are the product's own pieces: the prompt box, the send arrow, the heart, a hand cursor. Type blurs into focus word by word as it is spoken. Apps change under a liquid smear of the gradient while the window holds still. Shapes hand off to each other: the wordmark turns out to sit in a prompt box, the send button becomes a loading flower whose star opens onto the finished app, a sign-up button becomes a database row, a laptop shrinks into a globe icon. The camera pushes into whatever is about to be pressed, and every press gets a visible pressed state.

Each sequence below is sampled at a fixed rate so its frames read as a flip book. Bars show how much the picture changes from one source frame to the next: tall bars are fast moves, flat stretches are holds. The same notes are in motion.md for reading as text.
