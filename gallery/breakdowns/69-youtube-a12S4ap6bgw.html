Craft

A twenty-nine second New Year spot with no voice, no people on camera and no background but white. It takes the oldest January line there is, 'New year. New you.', and makes it literal: the product gives you a new you, in the form of a hand-drawn self-portrait. A wall of portraits slides through, one face tries on a year of looks, and then that face goes where it will actually be seen, into Notion's avatar slot and a row of collaborators.

What is worth learning is the path the film takes from a brand style to a product. Notion's ink-drawn illustration style is already part of how the company presents itself. Here they are shown first as a crowd (it is a system that can draw anyone), then as one person choosing (you can make it yours), then in the real interface (this is where it lives), then across a team (others will see it). Each step is a few seconds long, and each is made of a move the viewer already knows from software: a carousel, a picker, a click, a facepile.

Story and structure

A seasonal cliché, taken literally

January ads use 'New year, new you' to sell gym memberships and planners. This film uses it and means it: here is a new you, drawn. The whole spot is built on the gap between the worn-out phrase and the literal product, and it never explains the joke. 'New year.' sits alone for over a second so the viewer finishes the line in their head before 'New you.' appears.

The middle section is where the New Year idea gets its warmth. One face tries on eight looks in under six seconds, and they can be read as a year compressed: a cheeky grin, a donut, sunglasses, glasses and a pencil, a frazzled bad day, a graduation cap. It is resolutions and their consequences in one person, and it ends on an achievement. The film does not say any of this. It just orders the gags so they make a little arc, which turns a feature demo (look how many options there are) into a character.

Then the story turns practical, quickly. The face becomes a profile picture, the profile picture joins a team, and only then does the film name the feature. By the time 'Create your new look with Notion Faces' appears, the viewer has already seen what the look is for.

  2. The donut: the holidays before the resolutions.

The world and its graphics

The brand's drawing style becomes the product

The world is almost nothing: a white page, Notion's own sans for the few words, black ink drawings and three accent colors. The drawings carry it. Every face is in one hand: thick black line, solid black hair, grey skin with a fine halftone stipple, simple dot and comma features, and expressions borrowed from cartoons (spiral eyes, heart eyes, a wink). Variety comes from hair, glasses and how dark the shading is, so more than a dozen different people still look like one family.

Color is used for one job. On the wall, about half the tiles are yellow, blue or red with the face cropped into a white circle, and the rest are pale grey with the whole drawing. The colored tiles preview the face as an avatar, the round crop it will have in the app, before the film has shown the app at all. After the wall leaves, color disappears and does not come back; the rest of the film is black on white.

This is the move worth copying. The illustration style was already part of Notion's brand; the film turns it into something the viewer can own. It shows the style as a system first, so that it reads as capable of drawing anyone, and only then narrows down to one face that is clearly a person choosing.

  1. Colored tiles crop the face into an avatar circle; grey tiles show the full drawing.

  2. One ink style, many people: hair, glasses and shading do the variety.

  3. The halftone stipple on the skin, the only texture in the film.

Showing the product

Show the feature where it lives, not where it's made

The portrait maker itself never appears. There is no editor, no panel of options and no 'choose your hair' screen. The try-on sequence implies the options, and the product shots go straight to the destination: the circle at the top of a page where your face will actually be seen.

The before is Notion's real default, a grey letter in a white circle. Anyone who uses the app has seen that 'N' (or their own initial) and knows how blank it feels. A cursor clicks it, the button gives slightly, and the portrait pops in. Then the film widens to a facepile beside 'Share' and a comment icon, where Y, J, M and A sit as the same blank initials and then turn into faces, left to right. One click is the personal benefit; the row of faces is the social one, and it quietly suggests the invitation in the description to create and share.

Both UI moments are small and short, about two seconds each, and they are built from moves people already know: a press, a stack of avatars, a placeholder replaced by a picture. Nothing needs a caption.

  1. The default 'N' avatar, with the cursor on its way.

  2. After one click: the portrait in the circle.

  3. Initials Y, J, M, A beside 'Share': a page everyone has seen.

  4. The initials have become faces, the team has joined in.

How the frames work together

One page, one direction, many scales

The cut detector found no cuts, and in a sense it is right: the white page never changes, so even the four instant changes (to the avatar at 15.9, to the headline at 21.5, to 'Think it.' at 24.1 and to the logo at 25.25) read as the page updating, not as new shots. The face-to-avatar change is placed carefully: the circle appears in the same spot and at about the same size as the face it replaces, so the eye does not have to move.

Motion keeps one direction. The title is pushed out to the left, the wall arrives from the right, steps left a column and leaves to the left, and the single face enters from the right. It feels like swiping through a carousel, which suits a film about choosing a look.

The real variety is in scale. The film goes from a crowd filling the frame, to one face large and alone, to that face as a small avatar, to a row of tiny faces in a strip of UI. Each change of scale is a change of meaning: everyone, you, you in the app, you with your team. The composition is centered throughout, with lots of white around the subject, so the one thing that matters is never in doubt.

  2. One face enters from the right, the same direction as the crowd.

  3. The same face, now a small avatar alone on the page.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Take the seasonal cliché literally

    Pick the phrase everyone uses at this time of year and find the version where your product makes it literally true. Show the first half of the phrase alone so viewers complete it before you do.

  2. Show the style as a system before it's personal

    Open with many examples of what the tool can make, varied but clearly in one hand, then narrow to one. The crowd proves range; the single example invites the viewer in.

  3. Order the options into a little life

    When you show a product's options, sequence them so they tell a tiny story (here a year, from a donut to a graduation cap) and let some changes stay on. A menu becomes a character.

  4. Use the real default as the before

    Start the product moment on the blank state users already know, like a grey initial in an avatar circle, and change it with one click. The contrast needs no explanation.

  5. Put the feature where it will be seen

    Skip the editor and show the result in its final slot: the avatar, then a row of collaborators. Viewers see the benefit, not the work.

  6. Let the crowd come back as the team

    Reuse characters from the opening in the closing UI, so the final shot closes a loop and the people the viewer met earlier become the coworkers.

  7. Keep one page and one direction

    Hold a single background and move everything the same way across it. Instant changes then read as the page updating, and the whole film feels like one continuous gesture.

Not the lesson here. There is no voice, no live action, no real customers and no tour of the portrait maker; the music is a bed that the picture is not cut to, so it is not a film to study for sound. Study it for turning an illustration style into a product in under thirty seconds.

Motion

There is no camera and no hard cut the detector can see: the whole film plays on one white page, and every change is either a slide or an instant swap. Two habits run through it. Things move right to left, as if someone were swiping through a carousel (the title is pushed off left, the wall steps and leaves left, the single face enters from the right). And the face changes by swapping whole drawings on a single frame, like flipping through options in a picker, while the UI moments use small, familiar interface moves: a button press, avatars stacking, initials turning into pictures.
