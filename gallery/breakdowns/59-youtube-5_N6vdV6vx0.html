How Two Friends Built a €1.3M Charity Platform

Lovable's founder story about WeGlow. Quinten used to text his father 'I'm lighting a candle for you' before every hospital checkup, and that habit became a platform for lighting candles and donating, which the film says has raised more than €1.3 million. Two interviews in one warm room are captioned word by word in the empty wall beside each speaker, and every graphic, from a single lit candle to a glowing globe and a field of candles, turns the name into light. The breakdown covers how two text bubbles bookend the family story, how the voices are assembled, how low hits mark each reveal, and 14 moves.

Craft

A two-and-a-half-minute founder story built from very little: two interviews in one room, a handful of family snapshots, an old map, a candle and one page of the product. Quinten's father, who the film says has lived with Parkinson's for 15 years, got a text from his son before every hospital checkup: 'I'm lighting a candle for you to wish you good luck'. The father suggested turning that into an app that raises money. The film is the path from that text to WeGlow, a platform the film says has raised more than €1.3 million.

What is worth studying is how one small family gesture organizes everything. The candle becomes the visual language (a wick catching, a word that flares, a glowing globe, a field of candles), and the text message becomes a frame: the son's bubble at 15 seconds is answered by the father's at 2:22, 'So nice for you guys. I'm so proud of you guys.' Around that, a word-by-word caption system set into the empty wall beside each speaker keeps a plain interview moving.

Story and structure

A text message, sent and answered

The plot is an ordinary startup arc: an idea, a partner, years of research, a real problem, money running out, a breakthrough, results. What makes it feel like a story rather than a pitch is that it starts and ends inside a family. The first thing Quinten tells us is his father's illness and the text he sent before every hospital checkup. The last thing he tells us is the text his father sent back.

The candle runs through the middle like a thread. It is first a sentence in a message (15 s), then the father's idea (19 s), then a real flame that catches on 'our nice idea of lighting a candle' (62 s), then lights spreading across a map of the nonprofits they visited, then a glowing globe, and finally a field of thousands of candles under the €1.3 million. Each time it comes back it is bigger, so the growth of the company is told as the growth of one gesture.

Notice where the film puts its biggest number. '€1,300,000' arrives at 2:03, and a business video would end there. This one keeps going for another thirty seconds to the father's reply and 'his idea to see it in reality'. The number is the proof; the father is the ending.

  2. The idea becomes a real flame as Quinten says it.

  3. One candle has become a field of them.

  4. The father's reply closes the loop: 'I'm so proud of you guys.'

Voices and script

A narrator for the joins, the founders for everything that matters

Three voices carry the script. An unseen narrator speaks in short third-person lines that move the story between chapters: 'This is Quinten and Jef', 'To bring it to life, Quinten called his longtime friend Jef', 'over the next five years', 'What started as a tradition between father and son'. The film never names the narrator. The voice sits in a similar pitch range to Jef's, so it may be him reading a script, but nothing on screen says so.

The two founders split the content by temperament. Quinten carries the family, the problem and the pitch, and nearly every emotional line. Jef brings the lighter and harder notes: the Spider-Man meme about meeting someone exactly like you, then 'we spent a lot of money and we spent a lot of cash into trying to build that software'. Interview audio is laid over pictures only where the words name something that can be shown: the class photo under 'gym class', the candle under 'lighting a candle', the field under '35,000 candles', the campaign grid under '100-kilometer runs'.

The two quoted messages are a fourth voice. Quinten says each one aloud while it types into a chat bubble, so the father's words reach us in his son's voice and his own text at the same time. And the narrator gets the one joke: 'But WeGlow wasn't built overnight. Well, not initially.' It is typed on a card with a long pause, and the music drops out for it.

The founders' English is left as spoken, with 'the Parkinson's disease', 'to, to launch' and 'where we built it for years', and the captions transcribe it faithfully. That roughness is part of why the lines read as theirs.

  2. Jef's Spider-Man meme line plays over the class photo.

  3. The narrator undercuts itself: 'Not . . .'

  4. The narrator returns for 'a tradition between father and son' over family photos.

The world and its graphics

Captions in the room, and everything made of light

The caption system is the film's main graphic idea. Every word appears as it is spoken, in small bold white sans, and it is set in the empty wall beside the speaker rather than in a subtitle band. The layout changes with the sentence: a narrow column beside Quinten's face for his father's diagnosis, one long line straight across the frame for 'Quinten and I spoke for the first time through gym class', scattered loose words when Jef talks about the money, single words one at a time when Quinten hesitates. Size carries emphasis. 'or really low cost' is set large across his chest, and Lovable's logo sits inline inside a caption. When he lays out the nonprofits' two options, the first stacks to his right and the second to his left.

Everything outside the interview room sits on one ground: a dark navy with soft brush-stroke texture and a vignette. Present-day snapshots drop onto it as cards with shadows. Things from the past sit on cream paper instead, the class photo and the old map of Holland and Belgium, so the two surfaces separate then and now without a caption saying so.

Light is the motif, and the name WeGlow is acted out again and again. The map fills with soft yellow glows as nonprofits are visited. The word 'WeGlow' flares orange for a few frames as it types on. The film's turn, 'and in one day', comes through a white bloom that grows out of Quinten's T-shirt. The website in the opening says 'Let's Glow Up the world together' beside a globe with orange points, and later a painted globe glows with tiny lights over Europe. The candle field that carries the final number has the same smeared, painted texture as the navy ground, so the light always belongs to one world.

The interview room helps. It is warm plaster, a wooden chair, a big plant and a green sofa, lit with soft light raking across the wall. Its ochres sit close to candlelight, and they contrast with the cool navy of the graphics, so every cut between them reads as a step between the people and the idea.

  4. A painted globe glows with tiny lights, echoing the website's globe.

  5. The selling point gets the largest caption.

Showing the product

The product as other people's campaigns

For a film made by a software company, there is very little software. Lovable itself is never shown working. It appears once as a small logo inside the caption 'we had it in one day on Lovable', and again on the end card. WeGlow's own product appears three ways, each kept human: the homepage on a laptop held up in a snapshot, two text messages drawn as chat bubbles, and one eight-second drift down the 'Discover campaigns' page.

That one screen shot is shot as an object, not a recording. The page is tilted, with a narrow band of focus and fine scan lines, as if a camera were filming a monitor. Nothing is clicked. What fills it is other people's fundraisers with real names and amounts ('Kasper zoekt Helden', 'Support Baba', 'Bike for Bart') while Jef talks about runs and rides across Europe. The product proves itself by what people have done with it.

  1. The homepage only appears inside a snapshot: 'Let's Glow Up the world together'.

  2. Lovable's single appearance before the end card, as a logo inside a caption.

  3. The campaigns page, filmed at an angle with shallow focus.

Music and sound

Low hits on the reveals, and music held back

The music sits well under the voice all the way through, usually 5 to 15 dB below it, and no music is credited. The tempo estimate from the stem is about 178 BPM with only a moderate pulse, and it could be double the felt tempo. The cuts do not follow it: 13 of 38 land near a beat, slightly fewer than chance would give. What is designed is the punctuation and the gaps.

Reveals are marked with deep low hits rather than with cutting on the beat. There is one on the cut to the childhood photo at 23.3, one on the white bloom at 88.3, one on each of the father-and-son photos at 111.4, 113.0 and 114.4, one on the cut to the candle field, one as '€1,300,000' lands at 123.2, and a strong one as the father's typing bubble pops up at 141.1. Two of them, the cut into the first interview at 7.5 and the bloom, are led in by a rising wash.

The gaps matter as much. The music stem falls to near silence for the narrator's 'Well... not initially' (about 68.8 to 70.3), which is what makes the line land as a joke. Jef's account of the money and Quinten's 'very hard time' play almost dry, and the bed comes back at about 82 s under 'but our mission was so bright', then runs at its fullest after the bloom. It thins out again before the family photos, leaving about five seconds of voice alone before the first thud. Under the end card the music plays alone with its top end cut away, soft and closed in.

Trust, warmth and craft

Why it feels true

The film's warmth comes from things that would be hard to fake. The family photos are ordinary snapshots: black and white, a kitchen apron, a sofa, a garden table. The two text messages are short and plain, and the father's reply is the kind of thing a parent actually types. The numbers are specific and modest in scale: 15 years of Parkinson's, five years of meetings, 35,000 candles. The campaigns on screen have real names and real euro amounts.

It also admits things a pure advert would hide. The narrator jokes that the company was not built overnight. Jef says they spent a lot of money, and Quinten says it was a very hard time and they could not afford a developer team. A camera rig is left blurred in the foreground of one wide. The founders speak in their second language and nobody smooths it over.

The sponsor shows restraint. Lovable gets one sentence and one inline logo in the body of the film. Because the company stays out of the way, the claim that WeGlow was rebuilt in a day lands as part of the founders' story instead of a feature list.

  1. An everyday family snapshot carries the emotional turn.

  2. A camera rig left blurred in the foreground.

  3. Jef admits how much money went into the first build.

Techniques to reuse

  1. Bookend with the same gesture, reversed

    Find the small ritual at the heart of the story and show it twice in the same visual form: once going out at the start, once coming back at the end. Here the son's text bubble is answered by the father's.

  2. Grow one motif with the company

    Pick one object from the origin story and bring it back larger at each stage: a line in a text, a single flame, lights on a map, a glowing globe, a field. The growth of the motif tells the growth of the company.

  3. Set captions in the room

    Place word-by-word captions in the empty space beside the speaker instead of a subtitle band. Shape them to the sentence (columns, long lines, single words) and size them for emphasis.

  4. Use left and right for a choice

    When a speaker lays out two options, build the first on one side of them and the second on the other, so the frame itself becomes the comparison.

  5. Let the narrator undercut itself

    Give the scripted voice one honest joke, type it on with a pause and pull the music out under it. A single admission buys trust for the claims that follow.

  6. Hold the music back for the hard part

    Play the struggle nearly dry and bring the bed back on the line where hope returns, then open it fully at the turn.

  7. Mark reveals with low hits, not beat cuts

    When the edit follows the speech, punctuate the key reveals (a photo, a number, a message) with a deep low hit instead of cutting to the music's grid.

  8. Show the product through what people did with it

    Instead of a feature tour, film one page of real users' work, slowly and at an angle, while someone describes what those people did.

Not the lesson here. There is no walkthrough of Lovable, no lower-third name titles, no credited music and no customers or nonprofit staff on camera. It is not a film to study for UI animation or product demos; the lesson is how one family gesture, a caption system and a light motif carry a plain two-person interview.

Motion

The film is mostly two founders talking in one room, and the motion lives in what is laid over and cut between them. Every line is captioned word by word in the empty wall beside the speaker, placed and sized by hand. Between interviews, snapshots and an old map sit on a dark navy ground with brush-stroke texture, and the transitions borrow the brand's idea of light: a wick catching, a word that flares, a white bloom, a field of candles.
